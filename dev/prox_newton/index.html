Proximal Newton

ProxNewton fits penalized generalized linear models,

\[F(\beta) = s\sum_i w_i\,\ell(\eta_i, y_i) + g(\beta), \qquad \eta = X\beta + o,\]

for a GLMLoss of ProximalBase (logistic, Poisson, Huber, or one of your own) and a separable penalty $g$, as glmnet does (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2010). Each step minimizes a quadratic model of the loss plus the penalty by coordinate descent, and a line search on $F$ makes sure the objective decreases. It also solves the graphical lasso, by QUIC; see The graphical lasso by QUIC.

The quadratic model

Expand each term of the loss to second order in the linear predictor around the current $\eta$:

\[\ell(\eta_i', y_i) \approx \ell(\eta_i, y_i) + \ell'(\eta_i, y_i)(\eta_i' - \eta_i) + \tfrac12 \ell''(\eta_i, y_i)(\eta_i' - \eta_i)^2 = \tfrac12\, \ell''(\eta_i, y_i)\,(z_i - \eta_i')^2 + \text{const},\]

with the working response $z_i = \eta_i - \ell'(\eta_i, y_i)/\ell''(\eta_i, y_i)$. With $\eta' = X\beta' + o$, the model of the loss is the weighted least squares loss

\[\frac{s}{2}\sum_i v_i\,\big(z_i - o_i - x_i^\top\beta'\big)^2, \qquad v_i = w_i\,\ell''(\eta_i, y_i),\]

the iteratively reweighted least squares of generalized linear models, with the curvatures floored at $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ so that the working responses stay finite. For the logistic loss $\log(1 + e^\eta) - y\eta$, $\ell' = \sigma(\eta) - y$ and $\ell'' = \sigma(\eta)(1 - \sigma(\eta))$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function; for the Poisson loss $e^\eta - y\eta$, $\ell' = e^\eta - y$ and $\ell'' = e^\eta$.

A step

  1. Compute the weights $v$ and the working responses $z$ at the current $\beta$, and update the least squares model in place.
  2. Minimize the model plus $g$ by coordinate descent (inner, a CDSolver), warm started from $\beta$, which gives $\hat\beta$. The linear predictor $\hat\eta = X\hat\beta + o$ comes from the residuals of the model, without a product by $X$.
  3. Backtrack along $d = \hat\beta - \beta$: try $t = 1, \beta_{\text{ls}}, \beta_{\text{ls}}^2, \dots$ and accept the first $t$ with $F(\beta + t d) \le F(\beta) + 10\,\varepsilon|F(\beta)|$, where $\eta + t(\hat\eta - \eta)$ gives the loss without a product by $X$. If $t$ falls below $10^{-10}$, the step is abandoned and $\beta$ kept.

The method stops when $|F(\beta) - F(\beta^+)| \le \mathrm{tol}\cdot\max\{|F(\beta^+)|, 1\}$, or after maxiter steps. For a convex loss and penalty, and with the quadratic model strongly convex, $d$ is a descent direction of $F$ unless $\beta$ is a minimizer, and with exact inner solves the method converges, superlinearly near the solution (Lee, Sun and Saunders, 2014).

Why it is faster

Coordinate descent on the loss itself (Losses for coordinate descent) uses a global bound on the curvature, $\sup\ell''$, which for the logistic loss is $1/4$ and is far above $\ell''(\eta_i)$ wherever the fitted probabilities are close to 0 or 1: its steps are short. It also evaluates $\ell'$ at every observation in every coordinate step. The quadratic model uses the curvature at the current point, and its coordinate steps are those of weighted least squares, which cost $O(n)$ multiplications without evaluating the loss. On the logistic lasso problems of the package's benchmark, momentum makes the inner solves 1.0 to 4.5 times faster. The Poisson loss, whose curvature is unbounded, can only be fitted this way.

Intercepts and paths

For a logistic or Poisson loss, ProxNewton takes an unpenalized intercept through the model (cdmodel(f, x; intercept = true)), and solve_path takes ProxNewton with the same sequential strong rule as coordinate descent. The path also stops once the fit explains a fraction devmax = 0.999 of the null deviance, as glmnet's does:

yb = Float64.(y .> 0)                                    # binary responses
m = ProximalSolvers.cdmodel((1 / n) * LogisticLoss(yb, X), SparseIterate(p); intercept = true)
λmax = lambda_max(m, NormL1(1.0), SparseIterate(p))      # at the fit with only the intercept
b0 = Float64[]                                           # the intercepts along the path
path = solve_path(m, NormL1(1.0), λmax .* 10 .^ range(0, -2; length = 50), ProxNewton();
                  callback = (k, λ, β, m) -> (push!(b0, ProximalSolvers.intercept(m, β)); false))

The graphical lasso by QUIC

For the graphical lasso,

\[F(X) = w\big(\operatorname{tr}(SX) - \log\det X\big) + \sum_{ij}\lambda_{ij}\lvert X_{ij}\rvert,\]

f a LogDetLoss(S, w) (or a scaled one) and g a NormL1 (a matrix of weights can leave the diagonal free), ProxNewton is QUIC (Hsieh, Sustik, Dhillon and Ravikumar, 2014), on a SymmetricSparseIterate or a dense symmetric matrix. With $W = X^{-1}$, the step $\Delta$ minimizes the second-order model

\[w\Big(\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(W\Delta W\Delta) + \operatorname{tr}\big((S - W)\Delta\big)\Big) + g(X + \Delta)\]

by coordinate descent over the free set, the entries that are nonzero or whose gradient exceeds the penalty, with up to $2(1 + \lfloor k/3\rfloor)$ passes at step $k$, and an Armijo line search keeps $X$ positive definite. The solution is block diagonal along the connected components of the graph of the entries with $w\lvert S_{ij}\rvert$ above the penalty, so each component is solved on its own. The method stops when the relative duality gap (see Duality gaps) is at most tol, which the gap of the result reports. X is the starting point when it is positive definite; otherwise, zero for example, the start is the diagonal solution $1/(S_{ii} + \lambda_{ii}/w)$. A Newton step costs a few Cholesky factorizations, where an iteration of ADMM costs an eigendecomposition.

Other smooth losses

ProxNewton also accepts a smooth loss f with ProximalBase.hashessian(f) == true. The loss supplies quadratic_model(f, x) and quadratic_model!(q, f, x). Its model must be a function that cdmodel(q, x) supports, such as LeastSquaresLoss, QuadraticFunction or KronQuadratic, possibly scaled. A custom smooth loss can return a QuadraticFunction with its Hessian and linear term. A Hessian trait alone does not make an unsupported model usable by coordinate descent.

The quadratic model is a function of the new point z:

\[q(z) = f(x) + \langle\nabla f(x), z-x\rangle + \tfrac12\langle z-x, \nabla^2 f(x)(z-x)\rangle,\]

up to a constant. The inner solve minimizes q(z) + g(z), warm started at x. The outer step is d = z - x; the penalty is evaluated at z, without translating it again. The model buffers are updated with quadratic_model!. The coordinate-descent model is refreshed after each update so that copied matrices and scalar fields cannot retain the previous Hessian.

The generic path uses an Armijo line search on f + g. It rejects nonfinite trial values, including a trial outside the domain of f. A failed line search leaves the last accepted iterate and reports converged = false. The specialized GLM and graphical-lasso algorithms above retain their own model updates and stopping rules.

For the generic path, convergence is checked against the gradient of the original loss at the current iterate. Its unit-step proximal fixed-point residual is compared with tol * max(1, norm(x, Inf)). The residual is zero when the proximal stationarity condition holds; for a convex objective this characterizes a global minimizer. CDResults.maxchange and tolerance report that residual and its threshold. gap is NaN, since a generic smooth loss need not provide a Fenchel dual.

The inner change rule is followed by a proximal-residual check on the quadratic model. When needed, its tolerance is tightened, sharing the original inner.maxpasses budget across these refinements. The original objective's residual remains the convergence check for the outer solve.

Dense vectors and SparseIterates are supported. Matrix models can use a SymmetricSparseIterate or a dense symmetric matrix with NormL1 or ZeroFunction. For matrix-weighted NormL1, transposed entry weights are averaged: their sum determines the penalty on a symmetric pair. Vector penalties must be separable. The quadratic models must have positive semidefinite Hessians for the inner coordinate-descent solves to have their usual interpretation. This generic path does not fit an extra intercept or construct a screened penalty path; the GLM model interface supplies those features.

A = [2.0 0.2; 0.2 1.0]
f = QuadraticFunction(A, [-1.0, 0.5])
x = zeros(2)
r = solve!(x, f, NormL1(0.1), ProxNewton(; tol = 1e-8))
r.converged, r.maxchange, r.tolerance

Settings

ProxNewton(; inner = CDSolver(; tol = 1e-8), maxiter = 100, tol = 1e-8, β = 0.5). solve! returns a CDResults whose passes sum the inner passes. The GLM path reports convergence when its outer stopping rule was met and every inner solve converged. The graphical lasso stops on its duality gap. The generic path stops on the proximal fixed-point residual described above.
