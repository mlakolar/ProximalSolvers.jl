Coordinate descent

CDSolver minimizes

\[F(x) = f(x) + \sum_k g_k(x_k)\]

one coordinate at a time, where the penalty separates over coordinates (the weighted ℓ1 norm, the elastic net, MCP, SCAD, the nonnegative ℓ1 norm, bounds) or over groups of coordinates (the group lasso). The loss $f$ is one whose structure coordinate descent knows (see Losses for coordinate descent), with cached residuals or matrix products: least squares, quadratics, Kronecker quadratics of matrices, square-root losses and generalized linear losses.

The coordinate step

Fix every coordinate but $x_k$. Let $G = \nabla_k f(x)$ be the derivative of the loss along the coordinate and $a_k$ its curvature, exact when $f$ is quadratic and otherwise an upper bound. For $a_k>0$, suppose that $f(x + t e_k) \le f(x) + G t + \frac{a_k}{2} t^2$ for all $t$. The step minimizes this bound plus the penalty of the coordinate:

\[x_k^+ = \operatorname*{argmin}_t \; G\,(t - x_k) + \frac{a_k}{2}(t - x_k)^2 + g_k(t) = \operatorname{prox}_{g_k/a_k}\Big(x_k - \frac{G}{a_k}\Big).\]

The scalar proximal operator is prox_entry(g, v, i, γ) of ProximalBase, so every separable penalty of ProximalBase works without code in this package: soft thresholding for the ℓ1 norm, $\operatorname{shrink}(v, \gamma\lambda_1)/(1 + \gamma\lambda_2)$ for the elastic net, firm thresholding for MCP, a clamp for bounds. For a quadratic loss the step minimizes $F$ exactly over the coordinate; otherwise it minimizes a majorizer, and in both cases $F$ does not increase. A coordinate with $a_k = 0$, which the loss does not depend on, goes to the minimizer of $g_k$ nearest zero.

For a convex loss and a convex separable penalty, under mild conditions, every limit point of coordinate descent that visits each coordinate at least once in every $T$ consecutive steps is a minimizer of $F$: for exact minimization over each coordinate (Tseng, 2001), and for the majorization steps of a loss with a bounded curvature (Tseng and Yun, 2009). The solver interleaves such passes over all coordinates with passes over the active ones, and stops only after a full pass, as described below. For MCP and SCAD, which are not convex, coordinate descent converges to a stationary point when the coordinate problems are convex, as they are for standardized columns (Breheny and Huang, 2011).

The lasso update and residual cache

For the running example $f(\beta)=\|y-X\beta\|_2^2/(2n)$ and $g(\beta)=\lambda\|\beta\|_1$, write $r=y-X\beta$. The derivative and curvature along coordinate $j$ are

\[G_j=-\frac{X_j^\top r}{n},\qquad a_j=\frac{\|X_j\|_2^2}{n}.\]

For a nonzero column $X_j$, substituting these quantities into the coordinate step gives

\[\beta_j^+=\operatorname{soft}\left( \beta_j+\frac{X_j^\top r}{\|X_j\|_2^2}, \frac{n\lambda}{\|X_j\|_2^2}\right), \qquad \operatorname{soft}(v,c)=\operatorname{sign}(v)\max\{|v|-c,0\}.\]

After a change $\Delta=\beta_j^+-\beta_j$, the residual is updated as $r\leftarrow r-X_j\Delta$. Therefore, a dense coordinate step costs $O(n)$ operations, without recomputing $X\beta$. For a sparse design, the update visits only the nonzero entries of the column. A zero column contributes nothing to the loss, and its coefficient is set to zero.

The scalar proximal step and the residual cache serve different purposes. The proximal operator handles the penalty, while the coordinate model uses the loss structure to compute and update its derivatives cheaply. A full proximal gradient step uses the same soft thresholding operation on all coordinates after computing the full gradient.

Getting started runs both algorithms on this objective. Checking a solution derives the lasso optimality conditions and its duality gap.

Coordinates of the iterates

The iterate fixes what a coordinate is.

  • On a SparseIterate, a coordinate is an entry. The iterate stores the entries that have been nonzero: these are the active coordinates.

  • On a SymmetricSparseIterate, the coordinates are the entries $(r, c)$, $r \ge c$, of the lower triangle of a symmetric matrix $\Theta$. A step on an off-diagonal coordinate moves $\Theta_{rc}$ and $\Theta_{cr}$ together, along $D = E_{rc} + E_{cr}$. Let $G$ be the derivative of the loss along $D$ and $a$ its curvature along $D$. A penalty on the matrix counts the entry twice, once in each triangle, so along the coordinate it is $\mu\, g_{rc}(t)$ with $\mu = 2$ ($\mu = 1$ on the diagonal), and

    \[t^+ = \operatorname*{argmin}_t \; G\,(t - t_0) + \frac{a}{2}(t - t_0)^2 + \mu\, g_{rc}(t) = \operatorname{prox}_{\mu g_{rc}/a}\Big(t_0 - \frac{G}{a}\Big).\]

    The weights of a weighted ℓ1 norm are indexed like the full matrix and must be symmetric.

  • On an AtomIterate, a coordinate is a group of entries, for the group lasso; see the next section.

Groups

For the group lasso, $g(x) = \lambda \sum_k w_k \|x_{G_k}\|_2$ with the groups of an AtomIterate, the step on group $G = G_k$ majorizes the loss over the block by a quadratic with curvature $L_k \ge \lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2_{GG} f)$ and minimizes the majorizer plus the group's penalty (groupwise majorization descent, Yang and Zou, 2015):

\[u = x_G - \frac{1}{L_k}\nabla_G f(x), \qquad x_G^+ = \operatorname{prox}_{(\lambda w_k / L_k)\|\cdot\|_2}(u) = \max\Big\{1 - \frac{\lambda w_k}{L_k \|u\|_2},\ 0\Big\}\, u .\]

For least squares, $L_k = s\,\lambda_{\max}(X_G^\top W X_G)$, computed on the first visit to the group without allocating, and kept for the rest of a path. A group at zero stays at zero exactly when $\|\nabla_G f(x)\|_2 \le \lambda w_k$, its optimality condition, which is checked first: $L_k$ is never computed for a group that does not move.

Any other group penalty of ProximalBase on the groups of the AtomIterate, such as GroupNormLinf or a sparse group lasso, takes the same majorized step through its own proximal operator on the group, prox_coordinate!, and a separable penalty takes it entry by entry. When $L_k = 0$ the loss does not depend on the group, which goes to the minimizer of the penalty. These steps work with the least-squares and quadratic models.

Passes and the active set

A pass visits a set of coordinates once each, in a uniformly random order drawn afresh for each pass (order = :random, from the global random number generator) or in the order $1, 2, \dots$ (order = :cyclic). A full pass visits every coordinate, or the candidates kept by screening (see Penalty paths and screening); an active pass visits the active coordinates only. The active set does not change during a pass: a coordinate that becomes zero stays stored until the end of the pass, when zeros are dropped.

The passes follow the strategy of glmnet (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani, 2010). The first pass is full. After a full pass in which some coordinate moved by more than the tolerance, active passes follow until one moves no coordinate by more than the tolerance; then a full pass checks all coordinates. The method stops after a full pass in which no coordinate moves by more than the tolerance, or after maxpasses passes, with a warning. Most coordinates of a sparse solution are zero and stay zero, and the active passes, which do most of the work, never visit them.

The stopping rule

The change of a coordinate in a pass is measured on the scale of the data. With relative = true, the default, the change of coordinate $k$ is $\delta_k = |\Delta x_k|\sqrt{a_k}$ and the tolerance is $\mathrm{tol}\cdot\sigma$, where $\sqrt{a_k}$ and $\sigma$ come from the model (coordinate_scale and response_scale).

For least squares, $f(\beta) = \frac{s}{2}\sum_i w_i (y_i - o_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2$, the scales are $\sqrt{a_k} = \sqrt{s}\,\|X_k\|_W$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{s}\,\|y - o\|_W$ (with the columns and the response centered when the model fits an intercept). Then $\delta_k = \sqrt{s}\,\|X_k \Delta\beta_k\|_W$ is the change of the fitted values that the step makes, and the rule reads

\[\max_k \|X_k \Delta\beta_k\|_W < \mathrm{tol}\cdot \|y - o\|_W .\]

It does not depend on the units of the data: rescaling $y$, a column of $X$ (whose coefficient then scales inversely) or the loss leaves it unchanged. The other models follow the same pattern:

Model$\sqrt{a_k}$$\sigma$
least squares$\sqrt{s}\,\lVert X_k\rVert_W$$\sqrt{s}\,\lVert y - o\rVert_W$
quadratic $s(\tfrac12 x^\top A x + b^\top x + c)$$\sqrt{s A_{kk}}$$\sqrt{s}\max_k \lvert b_k\rvert/\sqrt{A_{kk}}$
Kronecker quadratic$\sqrt{a_k}$$\max_k \lvert \partial_k f(0)\rvert/\sqrt{a_k}$
generalized linear, curvature bound $\bar\ell$$\sqrt{a_k}$$\big(s \sum_i w_i \ell'(o_i, y_i)^2/\bar\ell\big)^{1/2}$
$\lVert y - X\beta\rVert$$\lVert X_k\rVert/\sqrt{n}$$\lVert y\rVert/\sqrt{n}$
$\sqrt{x^\top A x + 2b^\top x + c}$$\sqrt{A_{kk}}$$\sqrt{c}$

The columns and the response are centered in the rows of least squares and of $\lVert y - X\beta\rVert$ when the model fits an intercept. For a quadratic with $A = X^\top X/n$ and $b = -X^\top y/n$, $\sqrt{A_{kk}} = \|X_k\|/\sqrt{n}$ and, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $|b_k|/\sqrt{A_{kk}} \le \|y\|/\sqrt{n}$: the scale is a lower bound on that of least squares. With relative = false, or when $\sigma = 0$, the rule is absolute: $\max_k |\Delta x_k| < \mathrm{tol}$.

With gaptol > 0, a duality gap certifies the solution where one is known: where the rule would stop, the relative gap is computed, and the passes go on with a tenfold smaller tol until it is at most gaptol; see Duality gaps.

Momentum

With the random order, the passes over the active set use Nesterov momentum. Let $x_1$ be the active values at a restart, and $x_{j+1}$ those after the $j$-th active pass that follows. That pass starts from the extrapolated point

\[z = x_j + \omega_j\,(x_j - x_{j-1}), \qquad \omega_j = \frac{j - 1}{j + 2},\]

instead of $x_j$: the first pass after a restart has no momentum, and the second has $\omega_2 = 1/4$. The cache of a built-in model, such as the residuals of least squares, $Ax$ of a quadratic or the linear predictor of a generalized linear loss, is affine in $x$, so it moves to $z$ exactly by the same combination of its two last values, at the cost of a few vector operations per pass; the square root of a quadratic recomputes its value, which is not affine. A model of another loss is initialized at $z$.

The momentum restarts ($j \leftarrow 1$) after every full pass, when the active set changes, and when the step of a pass points against it, $(x_{j+1} - z)^\top (x_j - x_{j-1}) < 0$: the gradient restart of O'Donoghue and Candès (2015). Full passes are never extrapolated, and a cache moved by the momentum is computed afresh before the next full pass, so the method stops on a plain full pass, and screening checks an exact gradient.

By default (momentum = nothing), momentum is used with the random order on a SparseIterate for least squares, generalized linear losses, quadratics and the two square-root losses. On CoordinateDescent.jl's benchmark it makes lasso paths 2.8 to 4.5 times faster and single solves at small penalties 1.3 to 2.2 times; on logistic lasso problems coordinate descent is 1.4 to 5.6 times faster with it. momentum = true turns it on for any model on a SparseIterate or a SymmetricSparseIterate, such as the Kronecker quadratic (off by default: on vectorized matrices with two nonzero entries it was 1.1 to 1.2 times slower, while on the D-trace loss of a symmetric matrix it is up to 1.3 times faster). The cyclic order can stall with momentum, so it refuses momentum = true.

Anderson acceleration

With the cyclic order, a pass over a fixed active set $S$ is a fixed map $T$, and the passes are the fixed-point iteration $x \leftarrow T(x)$. anderson = K extrapolates it (Bertrand and Massias, 2021). It records the values $x^{(0)}, \dots, x^{(K)}$ on $S$ after $K + 1$ consecutive passes with the same active set, forms the differences $U = [x^{(1)} - x^{(0)}, \dots, x^{(K)} - x^{(K-1)}]$, and takes the affine combination of the last $K$ iterates whose combined step $Uc$ is smallest:

\[c = \frac{(U^\top U + \epsilon I)^{-1}\mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1^\top (U^\top U + \epsilon I)^{-1}\mathbf 1} = \operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf 1^\top c = 1} \|Uc\|^2 \ \ (\epsilon \to 0), \qquad x_e = \sum_{i=1}^{K} c_i\, x^{(i)},\]

with $\epsilon = 10^{-10}\operatorname{tr}(U^\top U)$ for stability. The extrapolated point is kept only if $F(x_e) < F(x^{(K)})$, which needs the value of the loss (loss_value). A full pass or a change of $S$ starts a new history. The random order changes $T$ from pass to pass, so anderson is ignored with it, as on an AtomIterate. On the lasso designs of the benchmark, CDSolver(; order = :cyclic, anderson = 5) takes 1.6 to 2.6 times less time than the cyclic order alone at $0.02\lambda_{\max}$.

An unpenalized intercept

For least squares and the square-root loss, cdmodel(f, x; intercept = true) minimizes over an unpenalized intercept $\beta_0$ as well. For a given $\beta$, the best intercept of $\frac{s}{2}\|y - \beta_0\mathbf 1 - X\beta\|^2$ is $\beta_0 = \bar y - \bar x^\top\beta$, with the means $\bar y$ of $y - o$ and $\bar x$ of the columns of $X$. Substituting it centers the problem:

\[\min_{\beta_0} \frac{s}{2}\|y - \beta_0\mathbf 1 - X\beta\|^2 = \frac{s}{2}\big\|(y - \bar y\mathbf 1) - (X - \mathbf 1\bar x^\top)\beta\big\|^2 .\]

The model computes the means once and centers the columns on the fly, so $X$ is not copied: the residuals are $r = (y - \bar y\mathbf 1) - (X - \mathbf 1 \bar x^\top)\beta$, the derivative is $-s(X_k - \bar x_k\mathbf 1)^\top r$ and the curvature $s\|X_k - \bar x_k\mathbf 1\|^2$. intercept(m, x) returns $\bar y - \bar x^\top x$. A constant column is exactly zero once centered, and its coefficient goes to zero. The intercept is not available with observation weights.

Settings

CDSolver(; maxpasses = 100_000, tol = 1e-7, order = :random, relative = true, anderson = 0, momentum = nothing, rng = Random.default_rng(), gaptol = 0, gapsafe = false). The random orders are drawn from rng. The result, a CDResults, reports the passes, whether the stopping rule was met, the largest scaled change in the last pass and the tolerance it was compared with, and the relative duality gap when gaptol or gapsafe computed it.
