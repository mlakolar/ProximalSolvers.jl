Numerical behavior

This page explains what a successful solve does and does not promise. You can start using the package without understanding its internal algorithm code.

What does converged mean?

  • Coordinate descent (CD): the change during a full pass has fallen below the effective tol (for the square-root lasso, a duality certificate must also pass; see the square-root lasso section below). With relative=true this threshold is scaled by a response-dependent quantity, sqrt(s * sum(abs2, y)), formed so that the sum of squares and its product with the loss scale s may leave the range of the working type; the reported effective tolerance can differ from the value passed to the constructor. When relative scaling is active (relative=true and a positive response scale), each coordinate change is also multiplied by sqrt(s * a) for the squared norm a of its column, formed from the two square roots where the product s * a is zero, subnormal or overflowing for a finite a; a nonzero change times an infinite scale (a computed scale that overflows the working type) is refused with an ArgumentError. The coordinate step forms its threshold lambda / (s * a) without the product or its reciprocal where either is outside the normal range (with weights, outside that range lambda stands for the coefficient T(lambda) * w[k] rounded in T; inside it ProximalBase's prox_block forms its threshold from lambda and w[k]), and a column whose squared norm is not finite is refused with an ArgumentError that names the curvature. For an ElasticNet, in the normal range the step of a visit is ProximalBase's prox_block. At a visit to a column with a finite positive a where s * a or its reciprocal is below floatmin of the type that combines s with the working type, the step with lambda2 = 0 is the lasso's, and with lambda2 > 0 the visit is refused with an ArgumentError that names the coordinate, during the pass: the iterate passed to solve! is unchanged, and a borrowed RNG may have advanced. A visit to a flat column (all entries 0) sets its coordinate to 0 at the levels that solve! accepts (also for a NormL1 at a zero weight), and a visit to a column that is not flat whose a underflows to 0 is refused with an ArgumentError about the curvature, as for the lasso. With maxpasses = 0 no coordinate is visited. With relative=false, or with y = 0, the coordinate scale is not used. If the computed sqrt(s) * norm(y), after rounding to the working type, is not a normal floating-point number, the solve is refused with an ArgumentError before the first pass (rescale, or use relative=false, which can still end in the nonfinite objective refusal described below); a value within a rounding error of the limits can fall on either side. y = 0 uses the absolute threshold. tol times a normal scale can be subnormal or 0; the test change < threshold cannot hold for a threshold of 0, so such a run cannot converge by it and ends at maxpasses with a warning, unless an independent numerical failure (for example a non-finite coordinate step) stops it first with an error. Once the active passes since the last full pass have together visited 20 times as many coordinates as a full pass, the next pass is a full pass even when the active changes have not fallen below the threshold. Float32 rounding of the coefficients can also keep the largest change of every full pass at or above the effective threshold (the reported tolerance, against which the change is compared after the scaling described above when relative scaling is active); the run then ends at maxpasses without converging, with the warning. A threshold well above that rounding level avoids this; Float64 data has a much lower rounding level. For a group lasso on a BlockIterate, the change is that of a whole group; see Group lasso on a BlockIterate.
  • Proximal gradient descent (PGD): the algorithm applies multiple stopping tests for relative coefficient change, objective change and a gradient/proximal residual. These combine by OR: any one of them can propose a stop, even if the others remain large, and a proposed stop counts only if the stationarity check of a stop passes. ftol = 0 switches the objective-change test off (see Strict accuracy).
  • Accelerated proximal gradient (APG): the same three tests and the same objective-increase stop as PGD. Its gradient/proximal residual is L ‖x - y‖∞, measured from the extrapolated point y rather than from the previous iterate, so it is a stopping quantity, not a KKT residual at x.

Neither converged=true is a certificate of mathematical optimality. For PGD and APG it means that a test proposed a stop and that the computed KKT residual ‖G‖∞ at the returned point is below max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) * max(λ, λmax), with λ the ℓ1 level of the penalty (for GroupNormL2, the group residual and scale; for NormLinf, the residual and scale of the generic check; see The stationarity check of a stop); it is a working-precision residual, not an exact-arithmetic certificate, and it does not bound the distance to a minimizer. The default gradtol = 1e-5 is not a specified number of correct digits. Tighten tolerances for results you intend to report, then compute the independent KKT residual. The Concepts and Getting started pages display that residual next to the default-PGD convergence flag. PGD's printed |g(x)| label is an internal stopping residual, not the value of the user's L1 penalty.

What the stopping tests imply and their measured limits (products that underflow or cancel, a cached residual of coordinate descent that is stale after a start far above the solution scale, Float32, ftol = 0) are on the page Limits of the stopping tests.

The stationarity check of a stop

PGD and APG verify a proposed stop; this section is the check for NormL1, ElasticNet and, with PGD, GroupNormL2 (the Groups item below), and other penalties use the generic stationarity check. At an iteration at which the x, f or gradient test holds (under ftol = 0, after the exact-stall rule below), the solver computes the minimal-norm KKT residual G at the accepted iterate x. For NormL1(λ) it is the element of ∇f(x) + λ ∂‖x‖₁ of least infinity norm, that is max(|∇fᵢ| - λ, 0) where xᵢ = 0 and |∇fᵢ + λ sign(xᵢ)| elsewhere, with λ = T(λ0), the penalty level converted to the coefficient type T. For NormL1(λ0, w) the coefficient of entry i is cᵢ = T(λ0) wᵢ, formed in T (the weights are a Vector{T}), so that G is max(|∇fᵢ| - cᵢ, 0) where xᵢ = 0 and |∇fᵢ + cᵢ sign(xᵢ)| elsewhere; a zero weight gives the coefficient 0 and the score |∇fᵢ|. For ElasticNet(λ1, λ2) (PGD and APG) it is the element of ∇f(x) + λ2 x + λ1 ∂‖x‖₁ of least infinity norm: max(|∇fᵢ| - λ1, 0) where xᵢ = 0 and |∇fᵢ + λ2 xᵢ + λ1 sign(xᵢ)| elsewhere, with the levels T(λ1) and T(λ2). The solver computes ‖G‖∞ with ProximalBase's kkt_violation(NormL1(T(λ0)), ∇f(x), x), kkt_violation(NormL1(T(λ0), w), ∇f(x), x) or kkt_violation(ElasticNet(T(λ1), T(λ2)), ∇f(x), x), passing the converted levels. An infinite entry of x gives a non-finite residual (Inf, or NaN when a NaN is read), so the stop does not count. Below, λ is the ℓ1 level of the penalty (λ of NormL1(λ), the largest coefficient maxᵢ cᵢ = T(λ0) * maximum(w) of NormL1(λ0, w), λ1 of ElasticNet). The stop counts only if

‖G‖∞ < max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) * max(λ, λmax),    λmax = α ‖Xᵀy‖∞,

where T is the element type of the coefficients and α is the loss scale (1 for a plain LeastSquaresLoss). The code forms τ = T(max(gradtol, 64 eps(T))) and the threshold τ * S in T; the normalized form ‖G‖∞ / S < max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) is the exact-arithmetic reading. In working precision the conversion to T and the product can round, or overflow to Inf for a huge gradtol, and a finite residual then passes against the infinite threshold. The check also requires that S = max(λ, λmax) is finite, that the product max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) * S, formed in T, is at least floatmin(T), and that ‖G‖∞ is finite. It does not require that product to be finite: for a huge gradtol it can be Inf, and a finite residual then passes. Otherwise x_converged, f_converged and grad_converged are all reported false, so converged=false, and the solve continues. The f_residual, x_residual and g_residual fields still report the quantities of the tests. What converged=true therefore says about the returned x:

  • The computed residual, from the gradient that the solver forms in working precision, is below the bound. It is not an exact-arithmetic certificate, because that gradient carries rounding error, and it bounds the KKT residual, not the distance to a minimizer.
  • The normalized inequality ‖G‖∞ / max(λ, λmax) < max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) does not change when the loss and the penalty are multiplied by the same positive factor, in exact arithmetic (the bound τ S itself scales with the factor). In working precision the outcome can change: the quantities are rounded, and the requirement that τ S is at least floatmin(T) can refuse a stop at a scale where α, λ, S and τ S are all representable. For example, for Float32 data X = [1], y = [1] at x = 0 with the default gradtol, α = λ = 1 passes and α = λ = 1f-35 does not, because τ S is below floatmin(Float32).
  • The factor 64 eps(T) is a floor on the relative residual. A gradtol below it, including gradtol = 0, gives the bound 64 eps(T) max(λ, λmax).
  • The exit at x = 0 with λ ≥ λmax has G = 0. A solve that starts at x = 0 with such a λ stops at the first iteration when a test proposes a stop there, S > 0 and τ S ≥ floatmin(T). Under ftol = 0 an unchanged iterate proposes nothing (see "An exact stall" below), so such a solve continues, and can end at maxiter with converged=false.
  • Where λ and λmax are both 0 (for example y = 0 and λ = 0), or where the bound is below floatmin(T), no stop passes. The solve then ends at maxiter unless another exit applies.
  • Weights. For NormL1(λ0, w), S = max(maxᵢ cᵢ, λmax); rounding is monotone, so the largest coefficient is T(λ0) * maximum(w). For w all ones the coefficients and S are those of NormL1(λ0). With all weights 0 the penalty vanishes, S = λmax and the check is that of least squares; with y = 0 as well, S is 0 and no stop counts, as above. A zero-weight coordinate is scored with its full gradient |∇fᵢ|, so a stop at a point where that gradient is at least τ S is not accepted, also where λ0 is large. A proposed stop adds two scans of w to the pass of kkt_violation (one for the maximum, one in the construction of NormL1(T(λ0), w)) and forms no array. Each cᵢ is a coefficient that solve! has checked: finite and, where T(λ0) and wᵢ are both positive, a normal number of T (a zero or subnormal product of positive factors is refused, and so is a subnormal T(λ0)); a zero level or weight gives cᵢ = 0. As above, a stop is refused where τ S is below floatmin(T).
  • Groups. For GroupNormL2(λ0, groups, w) with ProximalGradientDescent (a GroupNormL2{T} whose groups partition 1:p), the coefficient of group k is cₖ = T(λ0) wₖ rounded in T, and ‖G‖∞ is replaced by ProximalBase's group residual kkt_violation(g, ∇f(x), x) = max(0, maxₖ scoreₖ), with scoreₖ = ‖∇_G f + cₖ x_G/‖x_G‖₂‖₂ at a group with a nonzero entry and ‖∇_G f‖₂ - cₖ at a zero group (a zero weight gives the score ‖∇_G f‖₂). The scale is S = max(maxₖ cₖ, Λ) with Λ = maxₖ ‖[∇f(0)]_{Gₖ}‖₂ = α maxₖ ‖[Xᵀy]_{Gₖ}‖₂, the group counterpart of λmax (x = 0 is a minimizer iff ‖[∇f(0)]_{Gₖ}‖₂ ≤ cₖ for every k); each group norm is LinearAlgebra's norm, which scales the block before squaring where the square of its largest magnitude underflows to 0, so squaring the entries does not make Λ underflow to 0 at a scale such as α = 1e-300 (tested). It does not recover an entry of α Xᵀy that the product itself rounds to 0 or to a subnormal number. τ, the threshold and the guards are those above. For singleton groups with unit weights S is the S of NormL1(λ0), while the iterates and the residuals of the two penalties can differ by rounding (the scales agree where no entry of ∇f(0) has a subnormal square). As for NormL1, a stop that counts says that the computed residual is below the computed threshold, not that the residual in exact arithmetic is.
  • The elastic net. ElasticNet(λ1, λ2) on the loss α/2 ‖Xb - y‖² is the lasso with level λ1 on the augmented data X̃ = [√α X; √λ2 I], ỹ = [√α y; 0]: its loss ½‖X̃b - ỹ‖² equals α/2 ‖Xb - y‖² + (λ2/2)‖b‖², its gradient is ∇f(x) + λ2 x, and its λ̃max = ‖X̃ᵀỹ‖∞ = α‖Xᵀy‖∞ is the λmax above. The check with S = max(λ1, λmax) is therefore the lasso check on those data, and has the same properties: it is covariant under a common positive factor on α, λ1 and λ2 (in exact arithmetic), and it refuses every stop where the computed S = max(T(λ1), λmax) is 0, with λmax as the solver computes it. The scale does not contain λ2 or λ2‖x‖∞. With λ1 = 0 and y = 0, the computed S is 0 although x = 0 is the ridge solution: no stop counts, and the solve runs to maxiter with converged=false, as NormL1(0) does for y = 0 (unless another exit applies). An exact Xᵀy = 0 need not give a computed λmax = 0: in Float32, X = reshape(Float32[2^24, 1, -2^24, -1], 4, 1) with y = ones(Float32, 4) has Xᵀy = 0, but 2^24 + 1 rounds to 2^24 when the terms are summed in that order, so the computed λmax can be 1, and the check then works with that rounded value; a stop can then count at a point whose residual for the exact data is not small relative to the computed S. A start x0 with a finite f(x0) is accepted even if g(x0) overflows in T (for example a ridge term (λ2/2)‖x0‖² above floatmax(T)). An error raised in the step of an iteration (its line search, or the check that the accepted objective is finite) restores the last accepted iterate and is rethrown, as for NormL1. On a dense Vector or a one-dimensional SparseIterate, CDSolver accepts an ElasticNet and stops by its change test, as for NormL1; this check is not applied there.
  • The check bounds the residual relative to max(λ, λmax). A coordinate whose gradient is on a scale far below λmax can stay inaccurate when the check passes: for X = diag(1e6, 1), y = [1e6, 1], λ = 0 from a cold start with the default options, a review run reported convergence after 9 or 10 iterations with an independent relative KKT residual of a few 1e-6, while the coefficient of the flat column was almost 1 off. converged=true is not a guarantee of coefficient accuracy.

The check runs only at an iteration at which a test proposes a stop. PGD uses the gradient ∇f(x) that it holds. APG holds ∇f(y) at the extrapolated point, so it forms ∇f(x) from its cached product A x with the same gradient oracle, in preallocated buffers: one transposed product per check, counted in grad_calls (and not in f_calls). λmax is computed once per solve, at the first check, with one product Xᵀy that is not counted.

The gradient test itself has no absolute term. With r the gradient/proximal residual of the iteration, m the largest r so far in the solve, N = max(‖∇f‖∞, ‖s‖∞), with s the residual of the proximal step (for APG ∇f(y) and the step from y), and ρ = L eps(T) ‖x‖∞, the rounding resolution of L x, the test holds when

(r < gradtol * m  and  ρ < m)   or   (r < gradtol * N  and  ρ < N).

The comparisons use the floating-point products gradtol * m and gradtol * N. A residual of 0 holds only against a product that is positive: against m = N = 0 it does not hold, and neither when a product underflows to 0. A scale below ρ decides nothing. The objective test is abs(F - F') / abs(F) < ftol (see the table below) or the flat form, with no absolute term in the denominator.

With the default options, a solve with ftol > 0 can stop later than before: an objective change below ftol, or a flat objective, does not count as convergence while ‖G‖∞ is above the bound, and the solve can end at maxiter with converged=false. A Float32 solve with the default options can end on the objective-increase exit below, with converged=false and f_increased=true, where it used to stop on the objective test: the objective test proposes the stop, the check refuses it, and the computed objective then increases by rounding before the residual reaches the bound.

The generic stationarity check

A penalty without a dedicated check (today NormLinf) uses one check that needs only the penalty's proximal operator, so a new proximable penalty needs no convergence code of its own. At the accepted x, with t = 1/L (the accepted curvature), the solver forms p = prox_{t g}(x - t ∇f(x)) with the same ProximalBase value_and_prox! as the iterations, and the residual r = ‖x - p‖∞ / t, the ∞-norm of the proximal-gradient mapping. A proposed stop counts only if

r < max(gradtol, 64 eps(T)) * S,     S = max(‖∇f(0)‖∞, ‖∇f(x)‖∞),

with the threshold formed in T. It is refused when t is not finite and positive, when S or r is not finite, when the threshold is below floatmin(T) (so a solve with S = 0, as for X = 0 or at x = 0 when y = 0, does not report convergence), when the threshold does not exceed ρ = L eps(T) ‖x‖∞ (the rounding resolution of the gradient test), or when t times the threshold is below floatmin(T)/eps(T) (a step that subnormal rounding can lose at x = 0). Each proposed stop with a finite positive t costs one proximal evaluation, counted in prox_calls, and for APG one gradient at x, counted in grad_calls, before the call limits are compared.

A small accepted L makes t large and r a weaker measure of stationarity than with the true curvature; a penalty with curvature of its own far above L is the extreme case. converged = true is a working-precision indication, not a certificate, and it does not bound the distance to a minimizer.

PGD stops when the computed objective increases

With the default allow_f_increases=false in ProximalOptions, PGD has one more exit, which is not a convergence test: it stops at the first iteration whose computed objective F = f(x) + g(x), evaluated in the working precision, is larger than the previous iteration's F'. Such a stop does not mean the solve converged.

At the iteration where PGD stops, for whatever reason, the result's flags are evaluated as follows:

FieldValue at that iteration
x_convergednorm(x - x') < xtol * max(norm(x), 1) and the stationarity check passes; with ftol = 0, false when x == x' elementwise
f_convergedwith ftol > 0: abs(F - F') / abs(F) < ftol (the quotient is 0 for F == F' and Inf for F = 0 ≠ F'), or abs(F - F') < eps(abs(F) + abs(F')) (this form holds when F == F' and the sum is finite, and in the ordinary range, where F and F' are normal floating-point numbers and the sum is finite, it is about two units in the last place of F), and the stationarity check passes; with ftol = 0: false
grad_convergedthe gradient/proximal residual test with gradtol holds (see above) and the stationarity check passes; with ftol = 0, false when x == x' elementwise
convergedat least one of x_converged, f_converged and grad_converged, which are false together when the check fails
f_increasedF > F'
iteration_convergediterations == maxiter
stagnatedtrue when the stagnation condition of ProximalGradientDescent on Float32 data with ftol = 0 holds at that iteration (see Strict accuracy); false for AccProxGradDescent, for Float64 data and with ftol > 0. It can be true together with call_limit_reached, with iteration_converged, or at an iteration at which the callback returned true

So an objective-increase stop reports f_increased=true together with converged=false, unless one of the three tests also held at that iteration. Two other combinations are possible and not contradictory:

  • With ftol > 0, in the ordinary range (F and F' normal floating-point numbers, abs(F) + abs(F') finite), an increase smaller than about two units in the last place of F passes the objective test. The result then reports converged=true and f_increased=true if the stationarity check also passes, and converged=false with f_increased=true if it does not. With ftol = 0 the objective test is off, so an increase of any size reports converged=false unless the x or the gradient test held and the check passed.
  • A test that succeeds at the last allowed iteration reports converged=true and iteration_converged=true. An objective-increase stop at that iteration also has iteration_converged=true.

With allow_f_increases=true this exit is removed: the solve continues until a test holds or a limit is reached, and f_increased only reports the comparison at the last iteration.

Why rounding triggers it. For the supported least-squares lasso, with the backtracking line search's default Lexact = Inf, an accepted step does not increase the objective in exact arithmetic. A computed increase comes from floating-point rounding. Near a minimizer, the true change in F per step becomes smaller than the rounding error of evaluating F in the working precision (which depends on the data; on a small test problem of this package, a first-order bound is about 16 eps(T) relative), so the sign of the computed change is decided by rounding. At strict tolerances, whether a solve that has reached the neighborhood of a minimizer ends with converged=true or with an objective-increase stop can therefore depend on the last bits of the trajectory rather than on the accuracy of the point. Those bits can change with the starting point, the CPU and its vector width, the BLAS kernel, and how the code is compiled (for example, with coverage instrumentation). It matters mainly for tolerances near or beyond the working precision, and the package's tests see it most often with Float32. In both directions, assess the returned point with the independent KKT residual, not with converged alone.

Strict accuracy

With ftol > 0, including the default 1e-6, the objective-change test also holds when two successive computed objectives are equal or, in the ordinary range (both normal floating-point numbers, abs(F) + abs(F') finite), differ by about two units in the last place. That can happen before the point is accurate to the working precision, and the stationarity check of a stop then decides whether the solve stops. ProximalOptions(ftol = 0) switches the objective-change test off for ProximalGradientDescent and AccProxGradDescent: f_converged is false at every iteration. The objective-increase stop and the iteration limit are unchanged. So are the x and gradient tests, except that under ftol = 0 an iteration whose accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise sets neither x_converged nor grad_converged (see "An exact stall" below). The stationarity check applies to the remaining tests under ftol = 0 as well. ftol must be finite and nonnegative; a positive ftol that rounds to 0 in Float64 is refused, so that a tiny positive value does not silently switch the test off.

This combination is a test configuration, not a general recipe:

ProximalOptions(maxiter = 20_000, ftol = 0, allow_f_increases = true,
                xtol = 1e-300, gradtol = 1e-11)

On the package's benchmark cases (Float64 PGD and APG, Float32 APG, with 10 input draws each, on Julia 1.10 and 1.13 and two CPU models) it met the independently computed targets of a relative duality gap of 1e-8 in Float64 and 1e-5 in Float32. It is not a guarantee of accuracy on other problems, and converged=true is still not a certificate: check the returned point independently, for example with the bound below. With this configuration Float32 PGD reached its floating-point fixed point and met the independent 1e-5 target on the benchmark cases (in these probes, with maxiter = 10000; 10 input draws each, on Julia 1.10 and 1.13, one CPU model). With the stagnation stop (see "A stagnation stop" below), such a solve ends, before maxiter on those cases, at an iteration that meets its condition, with stagnated=true and converged=false. At an iteration whose accepted iterate is unchanged, the computed x and gradient tests can hold (the computed gradient residual can be 0), but under ftol = 0 the exact-stall rule (see "An exact stall" below) does not count them; between such iterations the iterate can still move. The computed residual is not an accuracy measure: the 1e-5 target was checked with the independent duality gap. These probes were made before the stationarity check of a stop was added (see The stationarity check of a stop); they are not repeated here for the current code.

  • allow_f_increases = true. With the objective test off, a computed increase of F still ends the solve under the default allow_f_increases=false, and reports converged=false unless the x or gradient test held. Near a minimizer such increases can be at the level of rounding (see above). In the benchmark probes, switching off the objective tests alone ended most solves at an increase, and the configuration above, which met the targets in those probes, enables increases.
  • xtol is not an accuracy criterion for PGD. PGD's step is scaled by 1/L, where L is the inverse step size, so the test norm(x - x') < xtol * max(norm(x), 1) bounds the proximal-gradient residual by about L * xtol * max(norm(x), 1), which depends on L, on the scale of x and on the penalty level λ, not on an accuracy target. In the benchmark probes with xtol = 1e-14, one Float64 PGD draw (on Julia 1.10) missed the target, and it ended on the x test. xtol = 1e-300 lets that test hold only for steps of norm below 1e-300 * max(norm(x), 1), which still includes a nonzero step that small (for example x = [1e-310] after x_previous = [0.0]). In the benchmark probes with these tolerances, all Float64 solves ended on the gradient test.
  • An exact stall under ftol = 0. An iteration whose accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise (by value) is no progress, not convergence: it sets neither x_converged nor grad_converged, and the solve continues, subject to the other tests, the limits and, with allow_f_increases=false, the objective-increase stop. With ftol > 0 such an iteration proposes a stop through the x test, and the stationarity check decides. A stall can end when the step size 1/L or, for APG, the extrapolated point changes from one iteration to the next; they need not change (for example with a zero step or α = 1). A solve that stays at an unchanged iterate until maxiter, with no iteration that meets the condition of the next item, ends with converged=false and iteration_converged=true; that reports no progress, not that the point is wrong (for x = 0 with a penalty level at or above norm(∇f(0), Inf), the point is the solution). For AccProxGradDescent, an extrapolated point y equal to x while x differs from the previous iterate is not a stall: its residual L * maximum(abs, x - y) is 0, so the gradient test holds when 0 < gradtol * N and ρ < N, or 0 < gradtol * m and ρ < m (see The stationarity check of a stop), and the stationarity check then decides.
  • A stagnation stop. Under ftol = 0, ProximalGradientDescent on Float32 data stops after an iteration in which both of these hold: the accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise, and the last rejection of that iteration's line search was decided by the computed curvature of the rejected step, which was finite and replaced the line search's curvature estimate L̂ (the retry's L is then min(Lexact, max(c, L / β)) for the rejected trial's L, so it need not equal the curvature c). The solve ends with stagnated=true and converged=false; x_converged, f_converged and grad_converged are false, and iteration_converged is iterations == maxiter. The stagnation check comes after tracing and after any callback call that is due at that iteration under print_every; it does not change the callback cadence, and a callback that returns true there can still stop the solve itself. A rejection that is not checked (a trial value that is not finite, a NaN estimate, a gradient estimate from a gradient with a nonfinite entry) or whose computed curvature is not finite does not count. A later rejection of the same iteration replaces an earlier one, and nothing carries over from an earlier iteration. An iteration whose first trial is already a zero step has no rejection and does not trigger the stagnation stop (other stops, such as a callback or a limit, still apply); this happens, for example, while L is so large that the first trial step rounds to a zero step, and at x = 0 with a penalty level at or above norm(∇f(0), Inf). An iteration whose accepted iterate differs from the previous one in any entry does not trigger it either, so a solve that keeps moving by rounding error, with no unchanged iterate, is ended by another stop or by maxiter. stagnated=true is not a convergence or accuracy certificate: the point can be inaccurate (a nearly interpolating problem with a small penalty level can stall with a duality gap above the 1e-5 target), and the iterate could still move at a later iteration if the solve continued. For Float64 data, for AccProxGradDescent and with ftol > 0, stagnated is false.

A check of a lasso solution. Let f(x) = s‖y - Xx‖²/2 with s > 0 and g(x) = λ‖x‖₁ with λ > 0 (an unweighted NormL1), let F = f + g, and let G be the element of ∇f(x) + λ ∂‖x‖₁ of smallest infinity norm, which is the independent KKT residual. Put k = norm(G, Inf) / λ. With the dual point θ = κ s (y - Xx), where κ = min(1, λ / norm(∇f(x), Inf)) and κ = 1 when ∇f(x) = 0, and D(θ) = ⟨θ, y⟩ - ‖θ‖²/(2s), the relative duality gap satisfies

\[\frac{F(x) - D(\theta)}{F(x)} \le 2k + k^2\]

in exact arithmetic, for s > 0, λ > 0 and F(x) > 0. This follows from F(x) - D(θ) = (1-κ)² f(x) + (1-κ) λ‖x‖₁ + κ⟨G, x⟩, 1 - κ ≤ k and F ≥ max(f, λ‖x‖₁). Evaluating k in Float64 from the returned x is an independent numerical check, not a rigorous floating-point certificate: the rounding errors of that evaluation are not bounded here.

Group lasso on a BlockIterate

CDSolver accepts a BlockIterate{T} with a GroupNormL2 penalty (see Supported problems); the coordinates of the solver are the groups. The pass schedule, tol, relative, maxpasses, the borrowed random number generator, the not-converged warning and the result type are those of the lasso. The result has kkt_checks = 0 and gap = NaN, placeholders and not computed certificates, and minimizer === x. A random order permutes the groups.

The group step

For a group k that is not flat (see below), with entries G and the current residual r, the solver uses a number ℓ_k > 0, computed as described below, that is meant to bound the largest eigenvalue of X[:, G]' * X[:, G] from above. A visit forms the trial point u = x[G] + X[:, G]' * r / ℓ_k in T, replaces the block by the proximal point of the penalty at u with the step 1 / (s ℓ_k), s the loss scale, and updates the residual for each entry of the block that changed. The change of the visit is the 2-norm ‖Δ_G‖₂ of the displacement of the block. A flat group has ℓ_k = 0 and takes the proximal point at the step Inf instead, without a trial point (see below). A group whose block does not change is not written, which keeps an inactive group inactive. A trial point or displacement that is not finite stops the solve with an ArgumentError that names the group, and the iterate of the caller is unchanged; a change or stopping scale that is not finite is refused as for the lasso.

The zero-group screen. When the block of a nonflat group is identically zero (whether or not the group is active), the visit first forms the correlations b = X[:, G]' * r and, if the two guards below allow, passes them to ProximalBase's prox_block! for the group with the step 1 / s. If the result is entirely zero, the visit ends with no change: nothing is written to the iterate or the residual, and a deferred ℓ_k is not computed. A zero block that the screen returns as zero forms no trial point, so it is not refused where the trial point b / ℓ_k of the full step would overflow. In exact arithmetic, the full step gives a zero block, for any ℓ_k > 0, exactly when the norm of b is at most λ w_k / s, and the screen tests the same inequality. The guarantee is one-sided: where the screen returns zero, the positive excess of the norm of b over the threshold λ w_k / s is confined to a rounding band around the equality (a screened zero can also be far inside the zero ball, for example b = 0). A nonzero screen result does not imply a nonzero full step: b / ℓ_k can underflow, so the full step can return zero where the screen does not. Two guards restrict the screen to cases where an entirely zero result is meant to come from the threshold test, up to such a rounding band:

  • the reciprocal 1 / s formed in the type S of the scale must be a finite positive normal number of S; it is Inf, 0 or subnormal otherwise;
  • either every correlation b_i is zero, or the largest |b_i| of the group is at least a floor, 2^53 floatmin(T), which is far above the underflow range of T. Otherwise ProximalBase's shrinkage of a very small correlation could round every entry of the result to zero although the norm of b exceeds the threshold, and a zero result would not certify the zero block.

Outside the guards, the screen is bypassed and the visit takes the full step, with the curvature computed if it is still deferred.

The curvature bound

ℓ_k is a bound on the stored values of X in T, formed as follows. The preparation runs after the residual is set, before the first pass and any draw from the random number generator, and only when maxpasses > 0; with maxpasses = 0 it is skipped and the preparation refusals below do not occur (the refusals of the inputs, the response scale and the final objective still apply). The preparation computes and checks ℓ_k for a flat group, for an ordinary one-column group and for a group that is not deferred; for a deferred group it checks only the bounds below, and ℓ_k stays NaN until the first visit that needs it.

  • Flat group. If every entry of X[:, G] is exactly 0, ℓ_k = 0, set during the preparation. The visit takes the minimizer of the penalty alone: a zero block when the level and the weight are positive, and the block unchanged when one of them is 0. Its stopping scale is 0.
  • Group of one column, with n the number of rows and n eps(T) ≤ 1/8. ℓ_k is computed during the preparation as the next floating-point number above a (1 + 2 n eps(T)), where a is the sum of squares of the column accumulated in T. The factor is a margin for the rounding of a, so ℓ_k is larger than the squared norm by a relative amount of about 2 n eps(T); the scalar lasso step uses a itself. The two sums are accumulated differently, so they can differ by rounding even before the margin.
  • Any other group, and a group of one column with n eps(T) > 1/8 (the Gram route). The Gram matrix Ĝ of the columns is formed in Float64 from a copy of the columns (for Float32 data too). With t the trace of Ĝ, m the size of the group and d the largest diagonal entry, the bound in Float64 is the next number above max(μ, d) + 4 (n + m²) eps(Float64) t, rounded up to T, where μ is the largest eigenvalue of Ĝ: all eigenvalues of Ĝ are computed and the largest is taken, for a group of m ≥ 2 columns. For a group of one column on this route, μ is the diagonal entry Ĝ[1, 1], with no eigenvalue computation and no eigenvalue model. The error terms that the margin covers are the rounding of Ĝ, products that underflow, and, for m ≥ 2, the eigenvalue computation. The first two follow from standard bounds and assume IEEE gradual underflow (subnormal numbers are not flushed to zero); the third assumes that the computed eigenvalues are exact eigenvalues of a nearby matrix, within a multiple of eps(Float64) times the trace, a model of the eigenvalue routine and not a proved bound for every input. This is the general rule; a group of two columns takes it outside the range of the next item.
  • Group of two columns (on the Gram route). With x and y the two columns, the entries a = ‖x‖², b = ‖y‖² and c = xᵀy of the Gram matrix are accumulated in Float64 in one pass over the stored columns, without a copy, and t = a + b is their computed trace. Where 2^-480 ≤ t ≤ 2^500, the bound in Float64 is the next number above (t + √((a − b)² + 4c²))/2 + (n + 4) eps(Float64) t, evaluated in Float64 and rounded up to T, with no eigenvalue computation. The first term is the largest eigenvalue of the computed Gram matrix in exact arithmetic. Inside this range the bound is proved, with no eigenvalue model: assuming IEEE binary64 arithmetic with rounding to nearest and gradual underflow, and n eps(Float64) ≤ 1/8, it exceeds the largest eigenvalue of the exact Gram matrix of the stored columns by at least ((6/7) n + 3) u t, with u = eps(Float64)/2, for any order of the summation. The margin is a quarter of that of the general rule. Under the same condition on n, finite Float32 data in a group that is not flat is inside this range. Float64 data is outside it where √t, the Frobenius norm of the two columns up to rounding, is below 2^-240 or above 2^250, and data with a NaN or infinite entry is outside it (t is then NaN or infinite). Outside the range the general rule decides, with its refusals.

The two rules for a group of two columns compute slightly different bounds: the margins differ, and the computed eigenvalue differs from the closed form in the last bits. The refusal categories and messages are those of the table below, but where the two bounds, or the steps s ℓ_k formed from them and their reciprocals, fall on different sides of a refusal threshold, the decision can differ from the one that the general rule would make. For the bound in Float64 before its rounding up to T, the thresholds are prevfloat(floatmin(T)) and floatmax(T): a bound at or below prevfloat(floatmin(T)) is refused as underflow, a bound strictly above it rounds up to at least floatmin(T), and a bound above floatmax(T) is refused as overflow. For s ℓ_k and its reciprocal the threshold is the smallest normal number of S (see below). Then a bound that the general rule refuses as overflow can be accepted (and then be refused by the step check, with the step message), a bound that it accepts can be refused as underflow, and a step decision can change in either direction. The gap between the two bounds scales with t, not with ℓ_k, so no fixed relative distance from a threshold is claimed.

Lazy computation. Computing the bound of a group costs a pass over its columns, and on the general rule a copy of the columns, their Gram matrix and, for two or more columns, an eigenvalue computation; a group that stays at zero may never need it. During the preparation, a group on the Gram route gets cheap bounds, formed in Float64 from the sums of squares q_c of its columns (n m operations, no products between columns): with t the sum and d the largest of the q_c, a lower bound lo = d (1 − 2 n eps(Float64)) − 2 n η and an upper bound hi = 4 t on the curvature that the rules above would store (η the smallest positive Float64; the bounds assume gradual underflow, and the upper one the eigenvalue model for a group that takes the eigenvalue computation: three or more columns, or two columns outside the range of their rule). Under these assumptions, the four conditions below exclude the refusals; a deferred group can still be refused during a pass through an eigenvalue routine that throws, or through a violation of the model. The curvature is left uncomputed (NaN in the model) only if the type S of the scale is Float32 or Float64, 4 (n + m²) eps(Float64) ≤ 1/16, t is finite, and all of these hold:

  1. lo ≥ 2 m floatmin(Float64), so that the trace underflow refusal is not made;
  2. lo ≥ 2 floatmin(T), so that the curvature underflow refusal is not made;
  3. hi ≤ floatmax(T) / 2, so that no overflow refusal is made;
  4. s lo ≥ 2 floatmin(S) and s hi ≤ 1 / (2 floatmin(S)), so that the step band check does not refuse.

The curvature is then computed, once per solve, at the first visit that needs it: a visit whose block is nonzero, or whose block is zero and which the screen above does not remove, and the band check is made there. A deferred group that stays at zero and is screened out at each visit never computes its curvature. If any condition fails or the bounds do not apply, the group is not deferred: the curvature and the band are computed during the preparation, as for the other groups, and refuse as below, so such a group may already have a curvature when it is screened. A one-column group with n eps(T) > 1/8 is on the Gram route and can be deferred. The bounds do not decide an eigenvalue routine that throws, or a largest eigenvalue that is not finite: these surface during the preparation for a group that is not deferred, and at the lazy computation, during a pass, for a deferred group. How much the lazy computation saves depends on how many groups fail the screen; see Cost of the lazy curvature.

The step of a nonflat group also needs s ℓ_k and its reciprocal to be at least the smallest normal number of the type S that combines s with T. This band check is made where the curvature is computed: during the preparation, or at the lazy computation. It cannot fail when S is wider than T, for example Float32 data with a Float64 loss scale, because the scale then converts to a finite nonzero Float32 and the curvature is a normal Float32 number; it can fail only when S is T itself. The scale need not have the data type: Float64 data X = fill(2.0^-500, 1, 1) with the Float32 scale nextfloat(0f0) has ℓ_k about 2^-1000, a normal Float64 number, and s ℓ_k is below the smallest normal Float64 number.

When the refusals happen. The refusals below throw an ArgumentError that names the group, and they leave x unchanged. Those that are predictable, because the curvature is computed during the preparation or the four conditions above hold, happen before the first pass, and the random number generator is unused. A curvature computed lazily is computed in the same way, and an exception of the eigenvalue routine (which is not caught), a largest eigenvalue that is not finite (both on the general rule), or a curvature or step refusal at that point (which the four conditions are meant to exclude) happens during a pass: x is still unchanged, but a random order may already have drawn from the generator.

The stopping statistic

With relative = true and a positive response scale, the change of a visit is ‖Δ_G‖₂ √(s ℓ_k), formed by the range-safe rule that the lasso uses for √(s a), and it is compared with tol times the response scale of the lasso. For a group of one column this is the lasso statistic up to the margin and rounding noted above. A flat group has scale 0, so its change is 0. With relative = false, or for y = 0, the change is ‖Δ_G‖₂ itself. A pass reports the largest change among the groups it visited, and a full pass whose largest change is below the threshold ends the solve with converged = true.

converged = true reports that change test only. It is not a certificate of optimality: compute the group KKT conditions from the data, as in the group lasso tutorial, where for ∇ = s Xᵀ(Xβ − y) a nonzero group needs ∇[G] + λ w β[G] / ‖β[G]‖ = 0 and a zero group needs ‖∇[G]‖ ≤ λ w. What the change test implies, and how far group CD stopped from a minimizer in the audit, is in What the change test of coordinate descent implies.

The pass schedule is that of the lasso with groups as the coordinates. A full pass visits every group and an active pass visits the active groups; after each pass the groups whose block is entirely zero become inactive. The forced full pass counts group visits: once the active passes since the last full pass have together visited 20 times as many groups as there are groups, the next pass is a full pass, whatever the changes.

Result and failure

The solve runs on a private copy of x. A finite completed run, including one that ends at maxpasses with the warning, replaces x by one same-layout copy of the final state; a refusal or numerical error before that leaves the values, the active groups and their order of x unchanged, though a random order may already have drawn from the generator. The entries of the groups that are inactive at the end are zero; their sign is not specified. After a solve, take activecoordinates(x) again, as for a SparseIterate.

Square-root lasso

CDSolver with SqrtLeastSquaresLoss (see Supported problems) uses the pass schedule, momentum and change test of the lasso with the loss scale s = 1: with relative=true and y ≠ 0 the threshold is tol * norm(y), and a change is multiplied by norm(X[:, k]); with relative=false, or y = 0, the threshold is the absolute tol and a change is not scaled.

The coordinate step

With a = ‖X_k‖², b = X_kᵀr, the centre c = old + b/a and d the distance of r from the span of X_k, the objective along coordinate k is √(a (z − c)² + d²) + λ|z|. Its minimizer is 0 for λ ≥ √a and shrink(c, λ d/√(a (a − λ²))) otherwise; the step uses the level T(lambda). It forms a, b and ‖r‖² in one pass over the column and the residual, and d² = ‖r‖² − b²/a; where that is below ‖r‖²/16 it forms d again as the norm of r − (b/a) X_k, because the subtraction loses accuracy there. Where a is below n floatmin(T)/eps(T), or ‖r‖² or that recomputed d² is below it or not finite (a column whose squares can underflow, a residual whose squares underflow or overflow, and r = 0), the step is formed in Float64 from the column and the residual scaled by powers of two. In that scaling a small entry can underflow or lose bits, which changes √a, b/√a and d by absolute amounts of at most 3n 2^-1074 times ‖X_k‖ or ‖r‖ (to first order); the error bound of the step, derived in the source, covers this (a tiny d can become 0). Where d/√(a (a − λ²)) or its product with λ is outside the normal range of T, the threshold is formed in Float64 without that product. At every visit, before any of these branches, a residual with an entry that is not finite is refused with an ArgumentError, and so are a column whose squared norm is 0 but which is not zero and a column whose squared norm is not finite; the coordinate and the residual are then unchanged.

The certificate

At a full pass whose largest change is below the threshold, the residual r̂ = y − Xβ is recomputed in Float64 over the nonzero coefficients, with the row magnitudes |y_i| + Σ_k |X_ik β_k|. The dual point is θ = t r̂/‖r̂‖ with t ≤ min(1, λ/max_k |X_kᵀr̂|/‖r̂‖), where the maximum is replaced by an upper bound and t is rounded down, so that ‖θ‖ ≤ 1 and ‖Xᵀθ‖∞ ≤ λ hold exactly; weak duality then gives P* ≥ θᵀy. The gap P − θᵀy is bounded by a sum of terms that are nonnegative in exact arithmetic, (1 − t)‖r̂‖ + Σ_k (λ|β_k| − t β_k X_kᵀr̂/‖r̂‖) plus (1 + t) times a bound on the error of r̂, without a difference of θᵀy and P. Each computed term carries a relative and an absolute rounding allowance, and the operations after the passes over the data round outward. The run is certified when the resulting upper bound of (P − P*)/P* (with a lower bound of θᵀy as denominator), rounded up, is at most 1e-4, and that bound is reported in gap. Not certified: a recomputed residual r̂ that is 0 in every entry with a nonzero coefficient (the direction is undefined; this is the usual outcome at an exact fit y = Xβ, and r̂ can also round to 0 where y − Xβ is not 0), a sum of squares of r̂ below n 2^-969 or not finite, and an intermediate value that is not finite. An exact fit whose r̂ is not 0 goes through the bound like any other point, and a certificate there is valid. The check also replaces the cached residual by r̂ rounded to T when every entry is representable in T, and minimum is evaluated from it at a stop of the check. A check costs one pass over X and one over its columns with nonzero coefficients. The proof of the allowance is a comment in src/cd/sqrtlasso.jl.

The rounding of r̂ in Float64 is of the order of eps(Float64) times the row magnitudes |y_i| + Σ_k |X_ik β_k| (larger than eps(Float64) ‖Xβ‖ where terms of a row cancel). Where these magnitudes dwarf the residual, for example where the fitted values do, that rounding enters the bound, and a point that is optimal can fail to be certified.

Tightening and the uncertified stop

A check that does not certify divides the threshold by 100 and the run continues, at most twice per solve and while tolerance/100 is finite, positive, below tolerance (a threshold that overflowed to Inf is not lowered) and at least a floor in the units of the threshold: 16 eps(T) norm(y) for a relative threshold (relative=true and y ≠ 0, where a change is scaled by norm(X[:, k])), and 16 eps(T) times the largest |β_k| at the check for an absolute one (relative=false, or y = 0), with no tightening at β = 0. The run stops with converged = false, gap = NaN and the warning coordinate descent stopped without a duality certificate when the certificate is not defined (above), when the full pass changed nothing, or when no tightening is left. With relative=true, y ≠ 0 and the default tol = 1e-7, Float32 data get no tightening, because 1e-9 is below 16 eps(Float32); with y = 0 the threshold and its floor are absolute, and a tightening can occur. With y = 0 and a nonzero start, P* = 0, so no relative bound can be certified unless β reaches 0; coordinate descent can approach 0 without reaching it and then stops uncertified. At an exact fit with a positive penalty, coordinate descent can stop at a point that minimizes P along every coordinate but not overall; such a run, and one that stops at the minimizer at an exact fit, are reported uncertified where the recomputed residual is 0, which is the usual outcome. An uncertified stop at the last allowed pass gets the uncertified warning, and passes == maxpasses alone does not tell it apart from the limit.

Working precision: Float32 and Float64

A supported solve uses a single floating-point working type T = Float32 or T = Float64 for the design matrix X, response vector y, and coefficient iterate. CDSolver accepts either a dense Vector{T}, a one-dimensional SparseIterate of T, or, for the group lasso, a BlockIterate{T}; ProximalGradientDescent and AccProxGradDescent accept only a dense Vector{T}. Mixed-precision arrays, such as Float32 coefficients with a Float64 design, are rejected. This is stricter than ProximalBase function dispatch, which may permit some heterogeneous calls without guaranteeing their numerical behavior.

Scalar-parameter exception. The NormL1 penalty level lambda may be a finite nonnegative real scalar of a different type, including a Float64 literal for a Float32 solve, and the loss scale in Scaled(LeastSquaresLoss(...), s) is supported in two forms: a positive finite Float32 or Float64, or a positive fixed-width integer or Bool that T represents exactly (2^24 but not 2^24 + 1 for Float32 data; 2^53 but not 2^53 + 1 for Float64 data). The solver checks that a penalty level, and a Float32 or Float64 loss scale, are representable in T without overflow or loss of a nonzero parameter to zero (a value that is subnormal in T is accepted). A loss scale of any other type (BigInt, BigFloat, Rational, Float16, Irrational or another Real) or an integer that T rounds is refused with an ArgumentError before any solver writes to the coefficients. This is an intentional narrowing of the supported API: those scales were accepted before the loss value and gradient came from ProximalBase's oracles (a BigFloat, BigInt or Rational{BigInt} scale made CD's scalar arithmetic BigFloat; an Int, Rational{Int}, Irrational, Bool or Float16 scale was used there as T(s)). Convert the scale explicitly where the conversion is finite, positive and representable in T, for example Float64(s) * LeastSquaresLoss(y, X); the error message suggests it when it passes that rule and, for an integer scale, only when Float64(s) equals s (for other types it notes that the conversion may round s). After the check, CD, PGD and APG use the loss scale as supplied, combined with T by Julia's promotion: a Float64 scale in a Float32 solve keeps its Float64 value (also where that value is subnormal in Float32). In CD, the coordinate step and the two stopping scales are formed from that value and rounded to T where they enter the iterate or the tolerance; an integer or Bool scale is used there as T(s). The loss part of CD's reported objective is formed by ProximalBase from the loss as supplied: it converts an integer or Bool scale to Float64, forms the product in the promoted type of the scale and T, and rounds the result to T. For an integer or Bool scale the check makes T(s) and Float64(s) both equal s, so CD's step and stopping computations and the objective use the same real scale. The arithmetic precision can differ: for Float32 data and the scale 3, CD's step and stopping arithmetic is in Float32 and the objective's product is in Float64; for Float64 data both are in Float64. This convenience does not enable a general mixed-precision model or guarantee that every intermediate operation uses T. Prefer explicit native scalars. Writing one(T) / T(n) chooses a native T scale: n is converted to T and the division is done in T, so the value can differ from the Float64 1/n. The solve uses that value, and the products with the loss scale are formed in T:

T = Float32
s = one(T) / T(n)
lambda = T(0.1)
f = s * LeastSquaresLoss(y, X)
g = NormL1(lambda)

All supplied X, y and coefficient entries must be finite. Finite inputs do not guarantee finite intermediate arithmetic. The ProximalBase mathematical kernels use selective overflow/underflow recovery for some representable results, but ProximalSolvers does not promise end-to-end recovery of every failed matrix product, curvature, step or objective. Unrecoverable numerical failures are handled as described below; a successful kernel recovery does not imply the solver can continue in every extreme-range case.

All three solvers take the least-squares value from ProximalBase's oracles (value_from_predictor and value_and_gradient_from_predictor! for PGD and APG, value_from_residual for CD), and PGD and APG take the coefficient gradient from value_and_gradient_from_predictor!. ProximalSolvers forms CD's coordinate correlations and updates and the λmax product of the stationarity check, and keeps the image X x (PGD, APG), the residual (CD), the steps, the line searches and the stopping rules. The exact accuracy and range statements are those of the section "Least squares at a predictor or residual that the caller holds" of the ProximalBase guide to the smooth functions; they are not restated here. What changes for the solvers:

  • CD takes the loss part of its objective from value_from_residual of its cached residual. ProximalBase recomputes exceptional values under the conditions its guide states (the "Value and rounding" section of the guide linked above); before, CD refused the finished solve where the sum of squares overflowed. The recovery holds for finite data and a value representable with ProximalBase's margin; near the overflow threshold its result can be Inf (the "Range" statement of the guide gives the conditions). CD returns an objective only when the loss plus the penalty is finite. Otherwise the finished solve is refused with a nonfinite objective ArgumentError and the starting point is unchanged; the sum can be non-finite where the recovered loss is finite, for example through an overflowing penalty value. Where the loss is below floatmin(T), the guide's bound for the loss is (n + 8) eps(T) |v| plus 2 nextfloat(zero(T)), with n the number of rows of X and v the exact value at the cached residual, so the reported loss part can be 0 or a small subnormal number.
  • PGD and APG take the value at a start and at each trial from value_from_predictor (PGD's start and its gradient refreshes use value_and_gradient_from_predictor!, and so does APG's stationarity check). A start or trial whose value ProximalBase recovers, under the conditions of its guide, is evaluated finitely where the sum of squares overflows in T; before, the sum of squares was formed in T without that recovery.
  • A start accepted in this way can have a finite value while Σ(z - y)² overflows in T. PGD and APG start their curvature estimate L at min(1, α‖X‖_F²) for the loss scale α (1 for an unscaled loss), with ‖X‖_F the solvers' upper bound on the Frobenius norm of X. The start is 1 when that product is at least 1 or X = 0, and a positive start that rounds below floatmin(T) is raised to floatmin(T). L is then multiplied by the line search's decrease factor (default 0.9) before each iteration and raised on a rejected trial. The start is the Frobenius bound α‖X‖_F² when that is positive and below 1, which exceeds the curvature α‖X‖₂² by the factor ‖X‖_F² / ‖X‖₂² (at most the rank of X), up to rounding and the floor; a problem whose positive Frobenius bound is far below 1 therefore starts below 1, and one whose bound is 1 or more starts at 1 (for example X = I(256)/16 with α = 1 has curvature 1/256 and starts at 1). For X = ones(5, 1), y = fill(1e160, 5), loss scale 1e-100, NormL1(0.0) and start 0, the curvature α‖X‖² is 5.000000000000001e-100, X has rank one, and both solvers start at 5.000000000000014e-100. APG ends with converged = true after 8 iterations, and with NormL1(0.1 λmax) after 7 (Julia 1.10.13 and 1.13.3 agree). PGD ends with converged = true after 10 iterations, and with NormL1(0.1 λmax) after 9 (Julia 1.10.12 and 1.13.3 agree). Its backtracking test squares the step norm, which overflows once a step exceeds about 1.3e154; a trial with a finite value and a step of finite entries is then decided on a scaled step (see "Steps whose squared norm overflows" under "Results and failures").
  • PGD and APG log no warning when they end at maxiter, so check converged and an independent KKT residual. CD logs a warning when a run ends with converged = false. CD solved the example above (converged = true, x = [1.0e160]); this is not a statement about extreme-scale problems in general.
  • A rescaling can serve as a workaround where it keeps the minimizers. For a NormL1 penalty, x = s u with s > 0 replaces X by sX and λ by sλ, multiplies the curvature by s², and turns a start x₀ into x₀ / s. On the example with s = 1e50 and level 1.0e60 replaced by 1.0e110, the curvature is above 1, the start is 1, and PGD and APG both returned converged = true, after 9 and 7 iterations. The transformation X′ = cX, y′ = cy, loss scale α/c², with the penalty unchanged, keeps the objective and the curvature α‖X‖², so it keeps the start up to rounding (with c = 1e-80 on the example, APG converged after 8 iterations and PGD after 10; Julia 1.10.12 and 1.13.3 agree).
  • The gradient is α times the rounded product Xᵀd with d = z - y for the image z = X x. An Xᵀd that overflows or underflows before the scaling is not recovered, also where α would bring the exact gradient into range.
  • Where z - y overflows with finite entries, value_from_predictor recovers the value, while value_and_gradient_from_predictor! returns a non-finite value and non-finite gradient entries.
    • At a start where z - y overflows, PGD takes the start's value from value_and_gradient_from_predictor! and refuses a non-finite one with ArgumentError("start from a point where f is finite"), also where value_from_predictor is finite. APG takes it from value_from_predictor and accepts the start where that value is finite; its first step then fails with ErrorException("the gradient step did not produce a finite objective"). Both leave x unchanged (Float32, X = [1;;], y = [3f38], loss scale 1e-44, NormL1(0f0), start [-3f38]). #328 tracks the message and the difference.
    • In PGD a trial is evaluated by value_from_predictor and the gradient at an accepted trial by value_and_gradient_from_predictor!. Where the line search decides from function values, the code accepts a trial on its finite value, so an accepted iterate can hold a non-finite gradient; this was not reproduced and is not covered by a test. Expected from the code only: if another step is attempted from such an iterate, x - ∇f/L is non-finite for every finite L, and for a penalty whose prox keeps non-finite entries (as NormL1 does) the trial values are non-finite, so the line search raises L (src/line_searches.jl:113-118) until next_curvature cannot increase it finitely and raises ErrorException("line search failed: the curvature estimate cannot increase finitely"); solve! then restores that iterate and rethrows (src/solve.jl:223-231); maxiter or another stop can end the solve before that step. In a Float32 probe with maxiter = 3000 (Julia 1.10.13 and 1.13.3), a trial whose gradient was non-finite was rejected, the step raised ErrorException("line search failed: nonfinite curvature estimate"), and the last accepted iterate was restored; this is not a guarantee for every setting.
  • In Float64, a subnormal squared step norm ‖d̂‖² of a PGD trial (a nonzero value below floatmin(Float64)) can make the line search's curvature estimates unreliable. Once the search uses gradients, it scales a gradient-difference product by 2/‖d̂‖², which overflows when ‖d̂‖² < 2/floatmax(Float64) (about 1.1e-308). A zero product is not scaled: it gives the estimate 0 and the trial is accepted, where scaling would give 0 · Inf = NaN. This does not make a solve converge at such scales: a solve can still end with converged = false or with a line-search error. One case is not handled: a nonzero product, or rounding noise in the function values, can give an infinite estimate, and when sum(abs2, d̂) underflows to 0 while ‖d̂‖² > 0 (every entry of d̂ below about 1.57e-162), the computed curvature of the step is not finite and does not replace that estimate. See LineSearches.BackTracking.

Float32 has less precision and may stagnate earlier than Float64. Some solver tolerances, residual reports and other scalar diagnostics are stored as Float64 even when the iterate is Float32; this is bookkeeping, not higher-precision optimization or an accuracy certificate. In particular, a tolerance tighter than the working precision does not guarantee corresponding coefficient accuracy. Assess the returned result with an independent KKT residual, rather than relying only on converged.

Results and failures

  • CD on dense coefficients calculates on private storage, avoiding a partially written dense result if a numerical failure occurs.

  • CD on a SparseIterate uses a private copy and commits the final set of active coordinates only after finite completion. This means the original coefficient storage is unchanged if the numerical solve fails. A pass can remove zeros from the active list and change the order of stored coordinates. The RNG can still advance.

  • PGD uses in-place dense arrays, and restores the last accepted iterate if a numerical step fails. Its counters and other internals are not transactional. Its backtracking line search decides each trial with TFOCS's local curvature estimate L̂, from function values and later from gradients (see LineSearches.BackTracking). Near a minimizer, rounding of f(x) - q(x) or of the difference of two gradients can inflate L̂ by orders of magnitude, which would shrink the step until x stops moving. So a rejected trial with a step d̂ = x - x' is decided again by the computed curvature of the step, c = m α ‖fl(X d̂)‖²/‖d̂‖², from one matrix-vector product X d̂, with m = 1 while the search uses function values and m = 2 once it uses gradients (TFOCS's gradient test is 2α ‖X d‖² ≤ L ‖d‖²). The trial is accepted when L ≥ c, and the search keeps its mode; otherwise it retries with L ← min(Lexact, max(c, L/β)) when c is finite, and with TFOCS's L ← min(Lexact, max(L̂, L/β)) when it is not. c is a computed value with no rounding certificate: when L is within rounding of the curvature along d̂, the decision can go either way, and a step nearly in the null space of X can have a computed c with a large relative error. For Float32 data the two squared norms are accumulated in Float64, where the square of a finite Float32 is exact, and two allowances are added to ‖fl(X d̂)‖ before squaring: 2√n p floatmin(Float32) for underflow of the results, and √n floatmin(Float32) ‖d̂‖₁ for subnormal entries of X that a thread flushing subnormals to zero (the calling thread or a BLAS thread) drops from the product. They assume that flushing affects only subnormal operands and results; they are not a bound on the total error of the product, and give no certificate. The curvature value compared with L, in Float64, is then positive when finite, so a product lost to underflow or flushing does not make it zero; the estimate used for a retry is that value rounded to Float32, which may round to Inf. TFOCS's update is kept when an entry of fl(X d̂) overflowed, when the calling thread flushes subnormals to zero (set_zero_subnormals(true)), and when d̂ has a subnormal entry, which a flushing thread could drop (for a trial whose step was scaled, d̂ is the scaled step of "Steps whose squared norm overflows" below). At ordinary scales the allowances are negligible: with round-to-nearest they leave the computed norm w = ‖fl(X d̂)‖ unchanged when their sum is below eps(w)/2. For Float64 data, over- or underflow of ‖fl(X d̂)‖² or ‖d̂‖² can make c infinite or NaN, which keeps TFOCS's update, or spuriously zero, which accepts the trial: with extreme scalings, ‖fl(X d̂)‖² can underflow, or ‖d̂‖² can overflow in the band described under "Steps whose squared norm overflows" below, so c can be zero although the true curvature is large. In the package's probes (seeded Float32 and Float64 designs, including p > n, near-duplicate columns, column scales over 10^±3, residuals orthogonal to the range of X and warm starts at the optimum), no accepted step increased the objective by more than 64 eps(T) |F|, evaluated in higher precision; this is a measurement, not a guarantee. A rejection is not checked, and keeps TFOCS's update, when the trial value is not finite, when the computed ‖d̂‖² is zero (the zero-step exit, which includes a tiny nonzero step whose square underflows), when L̂ is NaN, or when a gradient estimate comes from a gradient with a nonfinite entry. Each checked rejection costs one product X d̂. Accepted trials are not checked: a trial that a rounding-decided L̂ accepts below the curvature stays accepted. The Lexact exit (a finite-valued trial at L ≥ Lexact is accepted) and the zero-step exit are kept. A search that keeps rejecting until next_curvature finds no finite increase of L, or until TFOCS's update gives a nonfinite L, ends in an error with the last accepted iterate restored; that can happen when the curvature along the steps is above floatmax(T), unless TFOCS's estimate, Lexact or a zero step accepts a trial. With ftol > 0, a solve whose inflated L makes the step round to zero proposes a stop through the coefficient test (with ftol = 0 an unchanged iterate proposes nothing, as at the base). It used to report converged = true there; the stationarity check of a stop now refuses the stop unless the iterate meets the bound, and the solve continues. With ftol > 0 and allow_f_increases = false it can then reach the rounding regime of the objective and end on an objective-increase stop (f_increased = true); see "Why rounding triggers it" in PGD stops when the computed objective increases. Under ftol = 0 with the settings of Strict accuracy, a Float32 solve can reach its floating-point fixed point and end at the stagnation stop there, with stagnated = true and converged = false; without an iteration that meets its condition, the exact-stall rule applies and the solve can run to maxiter with converged = false.

    Steps whose squared norm overflows. Suppose the step d = x - x' of a trial has finite entries, the computed norm(d)^2 is Inf in T (a step above about 1.3e154 in Float64 and about 1.8e19 in Float32, which includes a step whose norm is itself above floatmax(T)), and the trial value f(x) is finite. The trial is then decided on the scaled step d̃ = s d, with s = 2^(-e) and e = min(exponent(max |dᵢ|), exponent(floatmax(T)) - 1), so that s is a normal number and the largest entry of d̃ is in [1, 2), or in [2, 4) when exponent(max |dᵢ|) is exponent(floatmax(T)) (1023 in Float64, 127 in Float32). A trial whose f(x) is not finite is not scaled: it takes the exit for a nonfinite trial value described above, as before. The local estimate L̂ is computed from d̃ and from f(x) and f(x') multiplied by s² (by shifting their exponents, since s² can underflow), with the products of d̃ and ∇f multiplied by s; in exact arithmetic it equals the estimate at d. The check of a rejected trial reads d̃, and after an accepted trial the solver state holds x - x' again. The trial is decided as at a representable scale, up to the rounding of the computed f and ∇f, overflow of the products ⟨d̃, ∇f(x')⟩ and ⟨∇f(x) - ∇f(x'), d̃⟩ before they are multiplied by s, underflow of an entry of the scaled step, of a scaled function value or of L̂, and, for Float32 data, the allowances of the check described above. Not changed:

    • a step with a nonfinite entry is not scaled, and its trial is decided as before;
    • a step whose squared norm is subnormal or underflows to zero is not scaled (#317);
    • an overflow of ⟨d̃, ∇f(x')⟩ (in the function-value estimate) or of ⟨∇f(x) - ∇f(x'), d̃⟩ (in the gradient estimate) before the factor s, or a NaN from overflowed terms of opposite sign, is not recovered by the scaling. L̂ is formed from that infinite or NaN product by the same expressions as for an unscaled step, with the scaled quantities, so it can be finite, infinite or NaN. For example, with finite f(x) and f(x'), a +Inf function-value product gives L̂ = L, and the trial is accepted without the check;
    • an overflow of ‖fl(X d̂)‖² for a representable ‖d̂‖² is handled by the check as before;
    • for Float64 data, in a narrow band around the overflow threshold of ‖d‖², norm(d)^2 can be finite while the sum of squares of d that the check forms overflows (in a probe with random d of length 10, 100 and 1000, within about 2 eps of the threshold); the step is not scaled, and its trial is decided as before;
    • for Float32 data, a subnormal entry of d̃ (nonzero, below floatmin(Float32)) makes the check keep TFOCS's update, as it does for any d̂ with a subnormal entry, and the check does not accept the trial. An entry of d with |s dᵢ| ≤ 2^-150 (half the least positive number) rounds to zero in d̃, which is not detected: it drops out of both sums of squares and of the product (d = [2^70, 2^-80] scales to [1, 0]). In exact arithmetic X d̃ is s times X d; the stored product can differ when an entry of d̃ has underflowed (for X = [0 1] and the d above, X d̃ = 0 while s X d = 2^-150). The allowances of the Float32 check are absolute, so they weigh more against ‖fl(X d̃)‖ the smaller it is, and they change c where it is of the order of the allowances.

  • APG handles a failed step in the same way. Its backtracking raises an error, after which the last accepted iterate is restored, when the next curvature estimate L/β is not a finite increase; a trial whose objective is not finite is never accepted. An extrapolated point whose objective or gradient is not finite restarts the momentum when the step uses momentum; without momentum the step fails, with the same rollback. Its line search accepts a step only when the least-squares bound √α ‖X(x - y)‖ ≤ (1 + 4eps) √L ‖x - y‖ is certified by conservative floating-point error bounds for the stored x and y, so it does not subtract nearly equal objective values and does not accept a step whose bound check is dominated by rounding. It first uses the difference of the cached images X x and X y, whose error is bounded through ‖X‖_F and the iterate norms; when that bound leaves the decision open it computes X(x - y) afresh, one extra matrix-vector product whose error bound is a relative term proportional to the step plus an absolute underflow term, and rejects a step that is still undecided. The norms and the bound arithmetic are carried as significand and binary exponent, so they do not overflow or round to subnormal values in T; the native products and their underflow enter only through the bounds. A curvature above floatmax(T) is never certified, so backtracking then ends in its error with rollback. These bounds assume IEEE arithmetic with gradual underflow and a matrix-vector product evaluated as dot products; the native products and the extrapolation are computed in T and enter only through the bounds. The extra product is needed more often in Float32, where the bounds are wider relative to small late steps; for data deep in the subnormal range, where the underflow terms can dominate the bounds, a step may need L far above the curvature, or the solve may fail with rollback. APG also requires max(n, p) + 4 < 1/(4 eps(T)) and n p + 3 < 1/(4 eps(Float64)); larger problems are refused before x is modified.

  • Reaching a pass or iteration limit is not convergence. CD can return a finite nonconverged solution with a warning.

Gap certificates are implemented only for the square-root lasso with CD (below); elsewhere gap=NaN is a placeholder. For more precise treatment of exceptional arithmetic, compaction, momentum, callbacks and test provenance, consult the technical contract notes.
