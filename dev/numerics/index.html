Numerical behavior

This page explains what a successful solve does and does not promise. You can start using the package without understanding its internal algorithm code.

What does converged mean?

  • Coordinate descent (CD): the change during a full pass has fallen below the effective tol. With relative=true this threshold is scaled by a response-dependent quantity, sqrt(s * sum(abs2, y)), formed so that the sum of squares and its product with the loss scale s may leave the range of the working type; the reported effective tolerance can differ from the value passed to the constructor. When relative scaling is active (relative=true and a positive response scale), each coordinate change is also multiplied by sqrt(s * a) for the squared norm a of its column, formed from the two square roots where the product s * a is zero, subnormal or overflowing for a finite a; a nonzero change times an infinite scale (a computed scale that overflows the working type) is refused with an ArgumentError. The coordinate step forms its threshold lambda / (s * a) without the product or its reciprocal where either is outside the normal range, and a column whose squared norm is not finite is refused with an ArgumentError that names the curvature. With relative=false, or with y = 0, the coordinate scale is not used. If the computed sqrt(s) * norm(y), after rounding to the working type, is not a normal floating-point number, the solve is refused with an ArgumentError before the first pass (rescale, or use relative=false, which can still end in the nonfinite objective refusal described below); a value within a rounding error of the limits can fall on either side. y = 0 uses the absolute threshold. tol times a normal scale can be subnormal or 0; the test change < threshold cannot hold for a threshold of 0, so such a run cannot converge by it and ends at maxpasses with a warning, unless an independent numerical failure (for example a non-finite coordinate step) stops it first with an error. Once the active passes since the last full pass have together visited 20 times as many coordinates as a full pass, the next pass is a full pass even when the active changes have not fallen below the threshold. Float32 rounding of the coefficients can also keep the largest change of every full pass at or above the effective threshold (the reported tolerance, against which the change is compared after the scaling described above when relative scaling is active); the run then ends at maxpasses without converging, with the warning. A threshold well above that rounding level avoids this; Float64 data has a much lower rounding level.
  • Proximal gradient descent (PGD): the algorithm applies multiple stopping tests for relative coefficient change, objective change and a gradient/proximal residual. These combine by OR: passing one can stop the algorithm, even if the others remain large. ftol = 0 switches the objective-change test off (see Strict accuracy).
  • Accelerated proximal gradient (APG): the same three tests and the same objective-increase stop as PGD. Its gradient/proximal residual is L ‖x - y‖∞, measured from the extrapolated point y rather than from the previous iterate, so it is a stopping quantity, not a KKT residual at x.

Neither converged=true is a certificate of mathematical optimality. On the small tutorial examples, default PGD typically produces only about 3–4 correct digits even if it reports converged=true. This is not a bound or expected accuracy on arbitrary problems. Tighten tolerances for results you intend to report, then compute the independent KKT residual. The Concepts and Getting started pages display that residual next to the default-PGD convergence flag. PGD's printed |g(x)| label is an internal stopping residual, not the value of the user's L1 penalty.

PGD stops when the computed objective increases

With the default allow_f_increases=false in ProximalOptions, PGD has one more exit, which is not a convergence test: it stops at the first iteration whose computed objective F = f(x) + g(x), evaluated in the working precision, is larger than the previous iteration's F'. Such a stop does not mean the solve converged.

At the iteration where PGD stops, for whatever reason, the result's flags are evaluated as follows:

FieldValue at that iteration
x_convergednorm(x - x') < xtol * max(norm(x), 1); with ftol = 0, false when x == x' elementwise
f_convergedwith ftol > 0: abs(F - F') / (abs(F) + ftol) < ftol, or abs(F - F') < eps(abs(F) + abs(F')) (this form holds when F == F' and the sum is finite, and in the ordinary range, where F and F' are normal floating-point numbers and the sum is finite, it is about two units in the last place of F); with ftol = 0: false
grad_convergedthe gradient/proximal residual test with gradtol holds; with ftol = 0, false when x == x' elementwise
convergedat least one of x_converged, f_converged and grad_converged
f_increasedF > F'
iteration_convergediterations == maxiter
stagnatedtrue when the stagnation condition of ProximalGradientDescent on Float32 data with ftol = 0 holds at that iteration (see Strict accuracy); false for AccProxGradDescent, for Float64 data and with ftol > 0. It can be true together with call_limit_reached, with iteration_converged, or at an iteration at which the callback returned true

So an objective-increase stop reports f_increased=true together with converged=false, unless one of the three tests also held at that iteration. Two other combinations are possible and not contradictory:

  • With ftol > 0, in the ordinary range (F and F' normal floating-point numbers, abs(F) + abs(F') finite), an increase smaller than about two units in the last place of F passes the objective test, so the result reports converged=true and f_increased=true. With ftol = 0 the objective test is off, so an increase of any size reports converged=false unless the x or the gradient test held.
  • A test that succeeds at the last allowed iteration reports converged=true and iteration_converged=true. An objective-increase stop at that iteration also has iteration_converged=true.

With allow_f_increases=true this exit is removed: the solve continues until a test holds or a limit is reached, and f_increased only reports the comparison at the last iteration.

Why rounding triggers it. For the supported least-squares lasso, with the backtracking line search's default Lexact = Inf, an accepted step does not increase the objective in exact arithmetic. A computed increase comes from floating-point rounding. Near a minimizer, the true change in F per step becomes smaller than the rounding error of evaluating F in the working precision (which depends on the data; on a small test problem of this package, a first-order bound is about 16 eps(T) relative), so the sign of the computed change is decided by rounding. At strict tolerances, whether a solve that has reached the neighborhood of a minimizer ends with converged=true or with an objective-increase stop can therefore depend on the last bits of the trajectory rather than on the accuracy of the point. Those bits can change with the starting point, the CPU and its vector width, the BLAS kernel, and how the code is compiled (for example, with coverage instrumentation). It matters mainly for tolerances near or beyond the working precision, and the package's tests see it most often with Float32. In both directions, assess the returned point with the independent KKT residual, not with converged alone.

Strict accuracy

With ftol > 0, including the default 1e-6, the objective-change test also holds when two successive computed objectives are equal or, in the ordinary range (both normal floating-point numbers, abs(F) + abs(F') finite), differ by about two units in the last place. That can happen well before the point is accurate to the working precision. ProximalOptions(ftol = 0) switches the objective-change test off for ProximalGradientDescent and AccProxGradDescent: f_converged is false at every iteration. The objective-increase stop and the iteration limit are unchanged. So are the x and gradient tests, except that under ftol = 0 an iteration whose accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise sets neither x_converged nor grad_converged (see "An exact stall" below). ftol must be finite and nonnegative; a positive ftol that rounds to 0 in Float64 is refused, so that a tiny positive value does not silently switch the test off.

This combination is a test configuration, not a general recipe:

ProximalOptions(maxiter = 20_000, ftol = 0, allow_f_increases = true,
                xtol = 1e-300, gradtol = 1e-11)

On the package's benchmark cases (Float64 PGD and APG, Float32 APG, with 10 input draws each, on Julia 1.10 and 1.13 and two CPU models) it met the independently computed targets of a relative duality gap of 1e-8 in Float64 and 1e-5 in Float32. It is not a guarantee of accuracy on other problems, and converged=true is still not a certificate: check the returned point independently, for example with the bound below. With this configuration Float32 PGD reached its floating-point fixed point and met the independent 1e-5 target on the benchmark cases (in these probes, with maxiter = 10000; 10 input draws each, on Julia 1.10 and 1.13, one CPU model). With the stagnation stop (see "A stagnation stop" below), such a solve ends, before maxiter on those cases, at an iteration that meets its condition, with stagnated=true and converged=false. At an iteration whose accepted iterate is unchanged, the computed x and gradient tests can hold (the computed gradient residual can be 0), but under ftol = 0 the exact-stall rule (see "An exact stall" below) does not count them; between such iterations the iterate can still move. The computed residual is not an accuracy measure: the 1e-5 target was checked with the independent duality gap.

  • allow_f_increases = true. With the objective test off, a computed increase of F still ends the solve under the default allow_f_increases=false, and reports converged=false unless the x or gradient test held. Near a minimizer such increases can be at the level of rounding (see above). In the benchmark probes, switching off the objective tests alone ended most solves at an increase, and the configuration above, which met the targets in those probes, enables increases.
  • xtol is not an accuracy criterion for PGD. PGD's step is scaled by 1/L, where L is the inverse step size, so the test norm(x - x') < xtol * max(norm(x), 1) bounds the proximal-gradient residual by about L * xtol * max(norm(x), 1), which depends on L, on the scale of x and on the penalty level λ, not on an accuracy target. In the benchmark probes with xtol = 1e-14, one Float64 PGD draw (on Julia 1.10) missed the target, and it ended on the x test. xtol = 1e-300 lets that test hold only for steps of norm below 1e-300 * max(norm(x), 1), which still includes a nonzero step that small (for example x = [1e-310] after x_previous = [0.0]). In the benchmark probes with these tolerances, all Float64 solves ended on the gradient test.
  • An exact stall under ftol = 0. An iteration whose accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise (by value) is no progress, not convergence: it sets neither x_converged nor grad_converged, and the solve continues, subject to the other tests, the limits and, with allow_f_increases=false, the objective-increase stop. With ftol > 0 such an iteration passes the x test. A stall can end when the step size 1/L or, for APG, the extrapolated point changes from one iteration to the next; they need not change (for example with a zero step or α = 1). A solve that stays at an unchanged iterate until maxiter, with no iteration that meets the condition of the next item, ends with converged=false and iteration_converged=true; that reports no progress, not that the point is wrong (for x = 0 with a penalty level at or above norm(∇f(0), Inf), the point is the solution). For AccProxGradDescent, an extrapolated point y equal to x while x differs from the previous iterate is not a stall: its residual L * maximum(abs, x - y) is 0, so the gradient test holds when gradtol > 0.
  • A stagnation stop. Under ftol = 0, ProximalGradientDescent on Float32 data stops after an iteration in which both of these hold: the accepted iterate equals the previous one elementwise, and the last rejection of that iteration's line search was decided by the computed curvature of the rejected step, which was finite and replaced the line search's curvature estimate L̂ (the retry's L is then min(Lexact, max(c, L / β)) for the rejected trial's L, so it need not equal the curvature c). The solve ends with stagnated=true and converged=false; x_converged, f_converged and grad_converged are false, and iteration_converged is iterations == maxiter. The stagnation check comes after tracing and after any callback call that is due at that iteration under print_every; it does not change the callback cadence, and a callback that returns true there can still stop the solve itself. A rejection that is not checked (a trial value that is not finite, a NaN estimate, a gradient estimate from a gradient with a nonfinite entry) or whose computed curvature is not finite does not count. A later rejection of the same iteration replaces an earlier one, and nothing carries over from an earlier iteration. An iteration whose first trial is already a zero step has no rejection and does not trigger the stagnation stop (other stops, such as a callback or a limit, still apply); this happens, for example, while L is so large that the first trial step rounds to a zero step, and at x = 0 with a penalty level at or above norm(∇f(0), Inf). An iteration whose accepted iterate differs from the previous one in any entry does not trigger it either, so a solve that keeps moving by rounding error, with no unchanged iterate, is ended by another stop or by maxiter. stagnated=true is not a convergence or accuracy certificate: the point can be inaccurate (a nearly interpolating problem with a small penalty level can stall with a duality gap above the 1e-5 target), and the iterate could still move at a later iteration if the solve continued. For Float64 data, for AccProxGradDescent and with ftol > 0, stagnated is false.

A check of a lasso solution. Let f(x) = s‖y - Xx‖²/2 with s > 0 and g(x) = λ‖x‖₁ with λ > 0 (an unweighted NormL1), let F = f + g, and let G be the element of ∇f(x) + λ ∂‖x‖₁ of smallest infinity norm, which is the independent KKT residual. Put k = norm(G, Inf) / λ. With the dual point θ = κ s (y - Xx), where κ = min(1, λ / norm(∇f(x), Inf)) and κ = 1 when ∇f(x) = 0, and D(θ) = ⟨θ, y⟩ - ‖θ‖²/(2s), the relative duality gap satisfies

\[\frac{F(x) - D(\theta)}{F(x)} \le 2k + k^2\]

in exact arithmetic, for s > 0, λ > 0 and F(x) > 0. This follows from F(x) - D(θ) = (1-κ)² f(x) + (1-κ) λ‖x‖₁ + κ⟨G, x⟩, 1 - κ ≤ k and F ≥ max(f, λ‖x‖₁). Evaluating k in Float64 from the returned x is an independent numerical check, not a rigorous floating-point certificate: the rounding errors of that evaluation are not bounded here.

Working precision: Float32 and Float64

A supported solve uses a single floating-point working type T = Float32 or T = Float64 for the design matrix X, response vector y, and coefficient iterate. CDSolver accepts either a dense Vector{T} or a one-dimensional SparseIterate of T; ProximalGradientDescent and AccProxGradDescent accept only a dense Vector{T}. Mixed-precision arrays, such as Float32 coefficients with a Float64 design, are rejected. This is stricter than ProximalBase function dispatch, which may permit some heterogeneous calls without guaranteeing their numerical behavior.

Scalar-parameter exception. The loss scale in Scaled(LeastSquaresLoss(...), s) and the NormL1 penalty level lambda may be finite real scalars of a different type, including a Float64 literal for a Float32 solve. The solver checks that they are representable in T without overflow or loss of a nonzero parameter to zero. After that check, CD, PGD and APG use the loss scale as supplied, combined with T by Julia's promotion (CD converts a scale to T when that promotion is not a concrete floating-point type, such as a custom Real without promotion rules): a Float64 scale in a Float32 solve keeps its Float64 value (also where that value is subnormal in Float32). In CD, the coordinate step, the two stopping scales and the reported objective are formed from that value and rounded to T where they enter the iterate, the tolerance or the result; an Int, Rational{Int}, Irrational, Bool or Float16 scale is used there as T(s). A BigFloat, BigInt or Rational{BigInt} loss scale makes the scalar arithmetic of CD that involves it (the step coefficient, the products of the loss scale in the two stopping scales, and the reported objective's product) BigFloat, at the precision current when the solve runs; the square roots of the stopping scales on their normal-range branch are formed in T. It allocates and is slow, and is not a fast path. This convenience does not enable a general mixed-precision model or guarantee that every intermediate operation uses T. Prefer explicit native scalars. Writing one(T) / T(n) chooses a native T scale: n is converted to T and the division is done in T, so the value can differ from the Float64 1/n. The solve uses that value, and the products with the loss scale are formed in T:

T = Float32
s = one(T) / T(n)
lambda = T(0.1)
f = s * LeastSquaresLoss(y, X)
g = NormL1(lambda)

All supplied X, y and coefficient entries must be finite. Finite inputs do not guarantee finite intermediate arithmetic. The ProximalBase mathematical kernels use selective overflow/underflow recovery for some representable results, but ProximalSolvers does not promise end-to-end recovery of every failed matrix product, curvature, step or objective. Unrecoverable numerical failures are handled as described below; a successful kernel recovery does not imply the solver can continue in every extreme-range case. For example, CD forms its reported objective from the sum of the squared residuals in the working type, times the loss scale: where that sum or product overflows, CD refuses the finished solve with a nonfinite objective error and leaves the starting point unchanged; where it underflows, the loss part of the reported minimum can be 0.

Float32 has less precision and may stagnate earlier than Float64. Some solver tolerances, residual reports and other scalar diagnostics are stored as Float64 even when the iterate is Float32; this is bookkeeping, not higher-precision optimization or an accuracy certificate. In particular, a tolerance tighter than the working precision does not guarantee corresponding coefficient accuracy. Assess the returned result with an independent KKT residual, rather than relying only on converged.

Results and failures

  • CD on dense coefficients calculates on private storage, avoiding a partially written dense result if a numerical failure occurs.
  • CD on a SparseIterate uses a private copy and commits the final set of active coordinates only after finite completion. This means the original coefficient storage is unchanged if the numerical solve fails. A pass can remove zeros from the active list and change the order of stored coordinates. The RNG can still advance.
  • PGD uses in-place dense arrays, and restores the last accepted iterate if a numerical step fails. Its counters and other internals are not transactional. Its backtracking line search decides each trial with TFOCS's local curvature estimate L̂, from function values and later from gradients (see LineSearches.BackTracking). Near a minimizer, rounding of f(x) - q(x) or of the difference of two gradients can inflate L̂ by orders of magnitude, which would shrink the step until x stops moving. So a rejected trial with a step d̂ = x - x' is decided again by the computed curvature of the step, c = m α ‖fl(X d̂)‖²/‖d̂‖², from one matrix-vector product X d̂, with m = 1 while the search uses function values and m = 2 once it uses gradients (TFOCS's gradient test is 2α ‖X d‖² ≤ L ‖d‖²). The trial is accepted when L ≥ c, and the search keeps its mode; otherwise it retries with L ← min(Lexact, max(c, L/β)) when c is finite, and with TFOCS's L ← min(Lexact, max(L̂, L/β)) when it is not. c is a computed value with no rounding certificate: when L is within rounding of the curvature along d̂, the decision can go either way, and a step nearly in the null space of X can have a computed c with a large relative error. For Float32 data the two squared norms are accumulated in Float64, where the square of a finite Float32 is exact, and two allowances are added to ‖fl(X d̂)‖ before squaring: 2√n p floatmin(Float32) for underflow of the results, and √n floatmin(Float32) ‖d̂‖₁ for subnormal entries of X that a thread flushing subnormals to zero (the calling thread or a BLAS thread) drops from the product. They assume that flushing affects only subnormal operands and results; they are not a bound on the total error of the product, and give no certificate. The curvature value compared with L, in Float64, is then positive when finite, so a product lost to underflow or flushing does not make it zero; the estimate used for a retry is that value rounded to Float32, which may round to Inf. TFOCS's update is kept when an entry of fl(X d̂) overflowed, when the calling thread flushes subnormals to zero (set_zero_subnormals(true)), and when d̂ has a subnormal entry, which a flushing thread could drop. At ordinary scales the allowances are negligible: with round-to-nearest they leave the computed norm w = ‖fl(X d̂)‖ unchanged when their sum is below eps(w)/2. For Float64 data, over- or underflow of ‖fl(X d̂)‖² or ‖d̂‖² can make c infinite or NaN, which keeps TFOCS's update, or spuriously zero, which accepts the trial: with extreme scalings, ‖fl(X d̂)‖² can underflow (or ‖d̂‖² overflow), so c can be zero although the true curvature is large. In the package's probes (seeded Float32 and Float64 designs, including p > n, near-duplicate columns, column scales over 10^±3, residuals orthogonal to the range of X and warm starts at the optimum), no accepted step increased the objective by more than 64 eps(T) |F|, evaluated in higher precision; this is a measurement, not a guarantee. A rejection is not checked, and keeps TFOCS's update, when the trial value is not finite, when the computed ‖d̂‖² is zero (the zero-step exit, which includes a tiny nonzero step whose square underflows), when L̂ is NaN, or when a gradient estimate comes from a gradient with a nonfinite entry. Each checked rejection costs one product X d̂. Accepted trials are not checked: a trial that a rounding-decided L̂ accepts below the curvature stays accepted. The Lexact exit (a finite-valued trial at L ≥ Lexact is accepted) and the zero-step exit are kept. A search that keeps rejecting until next_curvature finds no finite increase of L, or until TFOCS's update gives a nonfinite L, ends in an error with the last accepted iterate restored; that can happen when the curvature along the steps is above floatmax(T), unless TFOCS's estimate, Lexact or a zero step accepts a trial. With ftol > 0 and allow_f_increases = false, a solve whose inflated L used to make the step round to zero, so that the coefficient test reported converged = true, can instead reach the rounding regime of the objective and end on an objective-increase stop (f_increased = true); see "Why rounding triggers it" in PGD stops when the computed objective increases. Under ftol = 0 with the settings of Strict accuracy, a Float32 solve can reach its floating-point fixed point and end at the stagnation stop there, with stagnated = true and converged = false; without an iteration that meets its condition, the exact-stall rule applies and the solve can run to maxiter with converged = false.
  • APG handles a failed step in the same way. Its backtracking raises an error, after which the last accepted iterate is restored, when the next curvature estimate L/β is not a finite increase; a trial whose objective is not finite is never accepted. An extrapolated point whose objective or gradient is not finite restarts the momentum when the step uses momentum; without momentum the step fails, with the same rollback. Its line search accepts a step only when the least-squares bound √α ‖X(x - y)‖ ≤ (1 + 4eps) √L ‖x - y‖ is certified by conservative floating-point error bounds for the stored x and y, so it does not subtract nearly equal objective values and does not accept a step whose bound check is dominated by rounding. It first uses the difference of the cached images X x and X y, whose error is bounded through ‖X‖_F and the iterate norms; when that bound leaves the decision open it computes X(x - y) afresh, one extra matrix-vector product whose error bound is a relative term proportional to the step plus an absolute underflow term, and rejects a step that is still undecided. The norms and the bound arithmetic are carried as significand and binary exponent, so they do not overflow or round to subnormal values in T; the native products and their underflow enter only through the bounds. A curvature above floatmax(T) is never certified, so backtracking then ends in its error with rollback. These bounds assume IEEE arithmetic with gradual underflow and a matrix-vector product evaluated as dot products; the native products and the extrapolation are computed in T and enter only through the bounds. The extra product is needed more often in Float32, where the bounds are wider relative to small late steps; for data deep in the subnormal range, where the underflow terms can dominate the bounds, a step may need L far above the curvature, or the solve may fail with rollback. APG also requires max(n, p) + 4 < 1/(4 eps(T)) and n p + 3 < 1/(4 eps(Float64)); larger problems are refused before x is modified.
  • Reaching a pass or iteration limit is not convergence. CD can return a finite nonconverged solution with a warning.

Gap certificates are not implemented: gap=NaN is a placeholder. For more precise treatment of exceptional arithmetic, compaction, momentum, callbacks and test provenance, consult the technical contract notes.
