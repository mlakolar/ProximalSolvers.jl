Solver reference

ProximalSolvers — Module
ProximalSolvers

Solvers for minimize f(x) + g(x), built on the functions and proximal operators of ProximalBase: ProximalGradientDescent, AccProxGradDescent, MonotoneAccProxGradDescent, ActiveAccProxGradDescent and ADMMSolver for a proximable g, and coordinate descent (CDSolver, ProxNewton) for a separable one, all called through solve!. CondatVu handles convex linear composites f(x) + g(x) + h(D*x). Before its first release the package was called ProximalOPT.

source
ProximalSolvers.solve! — Function
solve!(x, f, g, method::ProximalSolver, options = ProximalOptions())

Minimize f(x) + g(x) starting from x, and overwrite x with the solution. For a convex linear composite, use solve!(x, f, g, h, D, CondatVu(), options) for f(x) + g(x) + h(D*x); see CondatVu.

For ProximalGradientDescent, AccProxGradDescent and MonotoneAccProxGradDescent, f is a smooth function of ProximalBase (issmooth(f)) and g a proximable one (isproximable(g)), possibly nonconvex, and x is an array of any shape that they accept, such as the p × k coefficient matrix of a LeastSquaresLoss with k responses and a NuclearNorm penalty; the result is an OptimizationResults. There x may also be a SparseIterate, a SymmetricSparseIterate (a symmetric matrix, as for KronQuadratic, LogDetLoss and IndBallL0(s; diagonal = false)), or for the group lasso GroupNormL2 an AtomIterate that shares the groups of g (AtomIterate(x, g.groups)): the iterates keep its type, zeros are dropped after each step, and products by the iterates touch only their stored entries or active groups. For ActiveAccProxGradDescent, x is a vector, a SparseIterate or an AtomIterate, and the result is an ActiveSetResults. For ADMMSolver, both f and g are proximable and the result is an ADMMResults. For CDSolver and ProxNewton, see their documentation.

The gradient solvers and ADMM also take a tuple of arrays with one element type, x = (x1, …, xK): a point of a product space, for functions of several blocks such as ProximalBase's SeparableSum(f1, …, fK) (fₖ on block k) or a penalty that couples the blocks. Norms and inner products are taken over all the blocks, and a tuple of one array computes exactly what the array does.

On a SymmetricSparseIterate, the gradient solvers prepare g by the same parameter rule as coordinate descent: average parameters that enter linearly, and require the other per-entry parameters to be symmetric (see the CDSolver method of solve!). The smooth function f is unchanged. For tuples with a SeparableSum penalty, the rule applies block by block; penalties that couple blocks pass unchanged and their symmetric-storage compatibility is the caller's responsibility.

Example

using ProximalBase, ProximalSolvers
A, b = [2. 0.; 0. 1.], [-1., -1.]
x = zeros(2)
res = solve!(x, QuadraticFunction(A, b), NormL1(0.1), AccProxGradDescent())

solve!(x, f, g, method::CDSolver)

Minimize f(x) + g(x) by coordinate descent, starting from x, a SparseIterate, a SymmetricSparseIterate, or an AtomIterate with the groups of a group penalty g; x is overwritten with the solution. A dense vector x is converted to a SparseIterate (an AtomIterate for a group penalty) and overwritten with the solution. Returns a CDResults. See CDSolver.

On a SymmetricSparseIterate, transposed entries are one variable. Solvers average asymmetric parameters that enter the penalty linearly: the weights of NormL1, NormL1Nonneg, ElasticNet (L1 term), CappedL1, LogPenalty, NormLq, Berhu, CheckLoss, EpsInsensitiveLoss and HingeLoss, and the coefficients c of Tilt and LinearFunction. Bounds of IndBox, patterns of IndPattern, centers y of SqrNormL2, centers c of Regularize, translations b of Translate, labels y of HingeLoss and references q of KLDivergence must be symmetric; otherwise an ArgumentError precedes any mutation. Normalization recurses through scaling, tilting, translation, regularization, box constraints and scalar Precompose. Inside MoreauEnvelope, Conjugate and linear constraints, every parameter must already be symmetric, including the constraint's coefficient a.

Symmetric or absent parameters keep the supplied penalty itself, and supplied arrays are never changed. The reported minimum uses the supplied objective. Unknown penalty types pass unchanged and must have symmetric per-entry parameters. Infinite weights are preserved; their proximal behavior remains that of ProximalBase, which some non-L1 families do not support.

source
solve!(x, f, g, method::CDSolver; continuation, continuation_tol, screening, gaptol)

With continuation = n > 0, x is set to zero and f + g is minimized along n penalty levels spaced geometrically from lambda_max down to the level of g, then at g itself, each warm started from the previous solution. The levels before g only give warm starts, so they are solved to the looser tolerance continuation_tol (√tol for the tolerance tol of method, by default) by the rule on the changes, and only g to the final accuracy; continuation_tol = method.tol solves every level to tol. As the levels of solve_path, the level of g stops on a relative duality gap of at most gaptol when f and g have one: PATH_GAPTOL = 1e-4 by default (or method.gaptol when it is positive), 1e-3 for a faster, looser solve, 0 for the rule on the changes with tol. The gap of the results reports the gap reached. With screening, each level is screened with the strong rule (see solve_path); the default screens except on a SymmetricSparseIterate, whose coordinate steps update the gradient everywhere, so that the optimality checks of screening cost more passes than they save. When the level of g is zero or at least lambda_max, f + g is minimized from zero directly, and so it is for a penalty without a level (ZeroFunction, IndBox, …), which has no path. As for solve_path, MCP, SCAD and their group versions need convex coordinate steps along the levels, and an ArgumentError says so when they are not. The symmetric-iterate parameter rule of the single solve! also applies to continuation.

source
solve!(x, f, g, method::LLA)

Local linear approximation at one level: the stages of method (see LLA) for the nonconvex penalty g, the first with the weights at x, each warm started from the previous solution, by reweight!. From x = 0 the first stage is the lasso; from an initial estimate, such as the unpenalized fit, LLA(stages = 1) is the one-step estimator of Zou and Li (2008). x is a SparseIterate, a SymmetricSparseIterate, or an AtomIterate with the groups of a group penalty, and holds the solution on return. Returns ReweightResults: iterations is the number of stages, converged whether the weights stopped changing, penalty the weighted lasso of the last stage and next that of the stage after it. On a SymmetricSparseIterate, both hold the averaged stage weights.

ProximalSolvers.ProximalOptions — Type
ProximalOptions(; kwargs...)

Options for solve!. The solver stops at the first of: a convergence test passes, maxiter iterations, time_limit seconds, a limit on the number of calls, the objective increases (unless allow_f_increases), or callback returns true.

Keywords

  • maxiter = 200: maximum number of iterations.
  • ftol = 1e-6: stop when |F(x) - F(x')| / (|F(x)| + ftol) < ftol, where F = f + g and x' is the previous iterate.
  • xtol = 1e-8: stop when ‖x - x'‖ < xtol * max(‖x‖, 1).
  • gradtol = 1e-5: stop when the relative or normalized optimality residual of Goldstein, Studer and Baraniuk (FASTA) is below gradtol. Not used by ADMMSolver, which has its own tolerances.
  • allow_f_increases = false: keep iterating when the objective increases.
  • store_trace = false: record an OptimizationState per iteration in the results.
  • show_trace = false: print the progress every print_every iterations.
  • extended_trace = false: also store the iterate and gradient in each state.
  • print_every = 1: how often to print and call callback; stored gradient traces include every iteration.
  • callback = nothing: a callable object called every print_every iterations with the trace (if store_trace) or the current state; return true to stop.
  • time_limit = Inf: maximum run time in seconds.
  • f_calls_limit = 0, grad_calls_limit = 0, prox_calls_limit = 0: stop once f, the gradient of f, or proximal operators (prox!) have been evaluated this many times; 0 means no limit. The counts are checked after each iteration, which always finishes, so a count can exceed its limit by the evaluations of one iteration. ADMMSolver uses only prox_calls_limit.
  • gaptol = 0: with gaptol > 0, stop when the relative duality gap (P - D) / |P| at the iterate is at most gaptol, checked every gapevery iterations, instead of on the ftol, xtol, gradtol tests and an increasing objective (or ADMM's residuals): a certificate that P = f + g is within gaptol ⋅ |P| of its minimum (see duality_gap for the functions that have a gap; for others gaptol is ignored, with a warning). Used by ProximalGradientDescent, AccProxGradDescent and ADMMSolver; the results report the final relative gap as gap.
  • gapevery = 10: how often to compute the gap when gaptol > 0. Each evaluation costs about two products with the design.

CondatVu uses these limits, tracing and gap options for a linear composite problem, with its own KKT residual tolerances instead of ftol, xtol and gradtol. Unsupported composite conjugates raise an error when gaptol > 0; a valid relative gap callback can be supplied instead.

The solvers work in the starting point's element type, including Float32 and Float64. Tolerances below working precision can produce stagnation or rounded residuals; they do not imply correspondingly accurate coefficients.

ProximalSolvers.SolverWorkspace — Type
SolverWorkspace(x, f, g, method, options = ProximalOptions())
SolverWorkspace(x, f, g, method::CDSolver)

Allocate reusable buffers for repeated solve! calls with a fixed loss, solver, options, iterate type and shape. Supports the three gradient solvers, ADMMSolver, and CDSolver. For coordinate descent, f may be an already configured CDModel, for example with an intercept. The workspace shares x with the caller.

solve!(workspace) starts from its current point. solve!(workspace, gnew) uses a new penalty of the same concrete type; group partitions must stay the same. This does not change the default penalty workspace.g supplied at construction. reinit! copies a new starting point into the existing storage. On symmetric iterates, the parameter rule of solve! is applied at construction and again for each solve, keeping the supplied workspace.g. Each call resets iteration history, counters and stopping tests. ADMM resets its dual variable and penalty parameter too, unless warm_start = true is passed to solve!, which keeps both from the previous solve.

Keep the loss data and group partitions fixed; create a new workspace to change them, the solver configuration, or the dimensions. Do not share one workspace between concurrent solves. Results share its minimizer and, for ADMM, its dual buffer, so copy those arrays before another solve if they must be retained. The workspace reuses solver-owned buffers; user loss/proximal operators, tracing, returned result objects, and optional refit/Anderson work may allocate.

Example

f = LeastSquaresLoss(y, X)
ws = SolverWorkspace(zeros(size(X, 2)), f, NormL1(0.1), AccProxGradDescent())
r1 = solve!(ws)
r2 = solve!(ws, NormL1(0.05))
reinit!(ws, zeros(size(X, 2)))
r3 = solve!(ws)
source
ProximalSolvers.reinit! — Function
reinit!(workspace::SolverWorkspace, x)

Copy a new starting point into a workspace without replacing its buffers. The type, element type, shape and, for sparse group iterates, partition must match. This also clears ADMM's dual warm start. Returns the workspace. Tuple blocks must have separate storage; cross-block source aliases are rejected before any block is overwritten.

Coordinate descent solvers

ProximalSolvers.CDSolver — Type
CDSolver(; maxpasses = 100_000, tol = 1e-7, order = :random, relative = true, anderson = 0,
           momentum = nothing, rng = Random.default_rng(), gaptol = 0, gapsafe = false)

Coordinate descent for f(x) + g(x), where g is separable (isseparable(g): NormL1, ElasticNet, MCP, SCAD, IndBox, …) or a group penalty such as GroupNormL2 or GroupNormLinf on an AtomIterate with its groups, and f is a loss whose structure solve! recognizes: LeastSquaresLoss, a least-squares GLMLoss, QuadraticFunction, KronQuadratic, or one of these scaled, as in (1/n) * LeastSquaresLoss(y, X).

Each pass visits the coordinates once, in random order (order = :random, drawn from rng) or in the order 1, 2, … (order = :cyclic). The first pass visits all coordinates. After a full pass that changes some coordinate by more than tol, passes over the active ones (the stored entries of the iterate) follow until one changes none by more than tol, and then another full pass. The method stops after a full pass in which no coordinate changes by more than tol, or after maxpasses passes. With relative, the change of coordinate k is measured as |Δxₖ| √aₖ, where aₖ is the curvature of f along the coordinate, and compared with tol times a scale of the data, so that the tolerance does not depend on the units of the data (for least squares, ‖X[:, k]‖ √s and ‖y‖ √s for f = s ‖y - Xβ‖² / 2).

With anderson = K > 0 and order = :cyclic, the passes over an unchanged active set are accelerated by Anderson extrapolation (Bertrand and Massias, Anderson acceleration of coordinate descent, AISTATS 2021): every K + 1 such passes, the iterates on the active set are extrapolated, and the extrapolated point is kept if it decreases f + g. The benefit depends on the problem and required accuracy. A random order changes the map of a pass from one pass to the next, which the extrapolation needs to repeat, so anderson is ignored with it, as on an AtomIterate. Strongly correlated columns still favor a random order.

With momentum, each pass over the active set starts from the extrapolated point xₖ + βₖ (xₖ - xₖ₋₁), with Nesterov's βₖ = (k - 1) / (k + 2), as in CoordinateDescent.jl 0.6. The momentum restarts after a full pass, when the active set shrinks, and when the step of a pass points against it (O'Donoghue and Candès, Adaptive restart for accelerated gradient schemes, 2015). Full passes are never extrapolated, so the method stops on a plain full pass. The cache of a built-in model moves with the point, at the cost of a few vector operations per pass; a model of another loss is initialized at the extrapolated point. By default (momentum = nothing) it is used with the random order on a SparseIterate for LeastSquaresLoss, GLMLoss, SqrtLeastSquaresLoss, QuadraticFunction and SqrtQuadraticFunction, and not otherwise. momentum = true asks for it with any model on a SparseIterate or a SymmetricSparseIterate, such as KronQuadratic; momentum = false turns it off. With the cyclic order it can stall far from the minimizer, so order = :cyclic refuses momentum = true.

The random order is drawn from rng, by default the task-local generator of Random.default_rng(), which draws exactly as the global generator does, so that Random.seed! makes a run reproducible. A generator of its own, such as Xoshiro(1), makes the passes reproducible without reseeding the global one, and separate generators give parallel solves independent streams. The solver keeps a reference to rng, so solves with the same solver advance the same generator; don't share one generator between tasks.

With gaptol > 0, the method stops when the relative duality gap (P - D) / |P| at x is at most gaptol, where P = f + g and D is the dual objective at a dual point built from the gradient (see duality_gap): a certificate that P(x) is within gaptol ⋅ |P| of the optimum. The rule on the changes then only decides when to compute the gap: its tolerance starts at gaptol (relative, instead of tol), the gap is computed where that rule would stop, and while it is above gaptol the tolerance falls by the factor the gap asks for and the passes go on. A gap costs one gradient over the candidate coordinates (all of them without screening; the paths confirm it with the full gradient of their optimality checks). The gap is taken as at least 4 eps |P|. A single solve! stops on the gap only with gaptol > 0; the paths of solve_path and the last level of continuation do by default (see PATH_GAPTOL). This needs a loss of a linear map with a known conjugate (LeastSquaresLoss, a GLMLoss with the squared, logistic or Huber loss, possibly scaled, with or without an intercept) and a convex penalty with a known conjugate (NormL1, NormL1Nonneg and ElasticNet with positive weights, GroupNormL2 on an AtomIterate with positive weights, and the sparse group lasso, SparseGroupNormL2 or its scaled form, on an AtomIterate, whose screening uses its dual norm as in Ndiaye, Fercoq, Gramfort and Salmon, 2016); with other losses and penalties the rule on the changes applies, and gaptol is ignored, except for the square-root losses (SqrtLeastSquaresLoss, SqrtQuadraticFunction), whose final point must have a relative gap of at most gaptol (1e-4 when gaptol = 0) to count as converged. With gapsafe = true and such a loss and penalty, each gap evaluation also screens the coordinates by the gap-safe rule of Fercoq, Gramfort and Salmon (Mind the duality gap: safer rules for the lasso, 2015), after every full pass: a coordinate it proves zero at the solution is set to zero and left out of the full passes for the rest of the run. On the lasso designs of benchmark/gap it removes most coordinates but saves less time than its gaps cost, so it is off by default. The relative gap at the end is the gap of the CDResults, NaN when it was not computed.

ProximalSolvers.ProxNewton — Type
ProxNewton(; inner = CDSolver(; tol = 1e-8), maxiter = 100, tol = 1e-8, β = 0.5)

Proximal Newton for f(x) + g(x), f a GLMLoss (or a scaled one) and g a separable penalty, as glmnet fits generalized linear models: each step minimizes the quadratic model of f at x, a weighted least-squares loss with the weights wᵢℓ''(ηᵢ) and the working responses ηᵢ - ℓ'(ηᵢ)/ℓ''(ηᵢ), plus g, by coordinate descent (inner) warm started from x, and then backtracks along the step on f + g, shrinking it by β, until the objective decreases. For the nonconvex MCP and SCAD, whose coordinate problems are convex only where the curvature of the model exceeds their concavity (ρ = 1/a, 1/(a - 1)), a step that does not decrease the objective is taken again with the proximal term ρ‖x - xₖ‖², which makes it a descent step without changing the fixed points (the stationary points of f + g). Without a duality gap, the steps stop when the objective changes by at most tol relative to max(|F|, 1) and the original problem is stationary to the matching accuracy: every exact coordinate step of f + g at x (and the intercept step, when fitted), measured as √aⱼ |Δⱼ| with aⱼ the curvature of coordinate j in the Newton model at x, is at most √tol * S, where S² = s Σᵢ wᵢ ℓ'(oᵢ,yᵢ)² / max(ℓ''(oᵢ,yᵢ), floor) at the offset. When all offset scores vanish, use S² = s Σᵢ wᵢ. Both sides scale by the square root of a positive loss and penalty scale; additive loss constants do not enter. This criterion does not bound objective or coefficient error. The method also stops after maxiter steps, with converged = false. Use with solve! on a SparseIterate; returns CDResults, whose passes sum the inner passes.

f may also be the model of a GLMLoss built with cdmodel(f, x; intercept = true), which fits an unpenalized intercept β₀ as well, as the intercept of the weighted least-squares model of each step: η = Xβ + β₀ + o. The model keeps β₀ (read it with intercept(m, x)) and starts the next solve from it. solve_path takes ProxNewton too.

For the graphical lasso, f a LogDetLoss(S, w) (or a scaled one) and g a NormL1 (a matrix of weights leaves, for example, the diagonal free), it is QUIC (Hsieh, Sustik, Dhillon and Ravikumar, QUIC: quadratic approximation for sparse inverse covariance estimation, 2014), on a SymmetricSparseIterate or a dense symmetric matrix X. Each step minimizes the second-order model w (tr(WΔWΔ)/2 + tr((S - W)Δ)) + g(X + Δ), W = X⁻¹, by coordinate descent over the free set (the entries that are nonzero or whose gradient exceeds the penalty), with up to 2 (1 + k ÷ 3) passes at step k, and the Armijo line search keeps X positive definite. The solution is block diagonal along the connected components of the graph of the entries with w |Sᵢⱼ| above the penalty, so each component is solved on its own. It stops when the relative duality gap (see duality_gap) is at most tol, which the gap of the results reports. X is the starting point when it is positive definite; otherwise (for example zero) the start is the diagonal solution 1/(Sᵢᵢ + λᵢᵢ/w).

Other smooth losses can provide ProximalBase.hashessian, quadratic_model and quadratic_model!. Their quadratic model is a function of the new point and must be supported by cdmodel, for example a QuadraticFunction, LeastSquaresLoss or KronQuadratic, possibly scaled. The inner coordinate descent minimizes that model plus g, and an Armijo line search rejects nonfinite trials. Dense vectors, SparseIterates, SymmetricSparseIterates and dense symmetric matrices are accepted. Vector penalties must be separable; the generic symmetric-matrix path supports NormL1 and ZeroFunction, following the symmetric-iterate parameter rule of solve!, as does QUIC.

For this generic path, the unit-step proximal fixed-point residual of the original objective must be at most tol * max(1, norm(x, Inf)) for convergence. The results report the residual in maxchange and the threshold in tolerance; gap is NaN. A failed line search reports no convergence. The quadratic-model buffers are reused, while their coordinate-descent caches are refreshed on each outer step. Generic losses do not receive the GLM's extra intercept or screened-path interface.

Proximal gradient solvers

ProximalSolvers.ProximalGradientDescent — Type
ProximalGradientDescent(; ls = LineSearches.BackTracking(), normalized = false, debias = 0, preconditioner = :none)

Proximal gradient descent (Parikh and Boyd, Proximal Algorithms, §4.2): x ← prox_{g/L}(x - ∇f(x) / L), with the step size 1/L chosen by the backtracking line search ls of TFOCS. Use with solve!. Symmetric iterates follow the penalty parameter rule described there.

With a nonconvex g, such as IndBallL0(s) (iterative hard thresholding), IndBallRank, NormL0, MCP or SCAD, the proximal step decreases the majorized objective in exact arithmetic. Stationarity results additionally require a proper lower semicontinuous penalty, suitable smoothness and bounded-step conditions, and bounded iterates. The traits alone do not verify those assumptions. Two options help iterative hard thresholding:

  • normalized = true starts the backtracking of each iteration from the normalized step of Blumensath and Davies (Normalized iterative hard thresholding: guaranteed stability and performance, 2010), instead of from the previous step: the minimizer of f along the gradient restricted to the support of x (all of it when x = 0). It costs one evaluation of f, and is exact for a quadratic f (least squares, KronQuadratic). For a tuple, the unrestricted gradient is used only when every block is zero; otherwise each block uses its own current support.
  • debias = k > 0 refits f on the support of x once the support has not changed for k iterations, with refit!, and keeps the refit if it lowers the objective, as MendelIHT does. It needs a loss that refit! supports and a vector, SparseIterate or SymmetricSparseIterate iterate.

With preconditioner = :diagonal, compute coordinate curvatures once using coordinate_lipschitz! and take entrywise steps 1 ./ (L .* D). Backtracking chooses the scalar 1/L in the D norm; the diagonal alone is not a global curvature bound. Optimality residuals and iterate tolerances use the coordinates sqrt.(D) .* x; duality gaps still certify the original objective. Zero curvatures use unit metric entries; negative or nonfinite curvatures are rejected. This option needs independently stored real floating point entries and a penalty with an array-step value_and_prox! method. Tuple blocks and SymmetricSparseIterate are not supported. Keep loss data fixed when reusing a workspace, which also reuses the metric.

source
ProximalSolvers.AccProxGradDescent — Type
AccProxGradDescent(; β = 0.5, α = 0.9, preconditioner = :none)

Accelerated proximal gradient descent (Parikh and Boyd, Proximal Algorithms, §4.3). Each iteration extrapolates y = x + k/(k+3) (x - x') and takes a proximal gradient step from y; the step size 1/L is found by backtracking, shrinking it by the factor β ∈ (0, 1). Before each iteration L is multiplied by α ∈ (0, 1], so that the step grows again where the curvature is smaller, as in TFOCS; α = 1 keeps L from decreasing. The runtime tradeoff depends on the number of accepted steps and rejected trials, so compare settings at matched accuracy.

Backtracking raises an error if roundoff prevents a representable step from satisfying the descent bound, or the next curvature estimate is not finite.

The momentum restarts whenever the objective would increase (O'Donoghue and Candès, Adaptive restart for accelerated gradient schemes, 2015), so the objective is nonincreasing. Use with solve!. With preconditioner = :diagonal, compute coordinate curvatures once using coordinate_lipschitz! and take entrywise steps 1 ./ (L .* D). Backtracking chooses the scalar 1/L in the D norm; the diagonal alone is not a global curvature bound. Optimality residuals and iterate tolerances use the coordinates sqrt.(D) .* x; duality gaps still certify the original objective. Zero curvatures use unit metric entries; negative or nonfinite curvatures are rejected. This option needs independently stored real floating point entries and a penalty with an array-step value_and_prox! method. Tuple blocks and SymmetricSparseIterate are not supported. Keep loss data fixed when reusing a workspace, which also reuses the metric.

source
ProximalSolvers.MonotoneAccProxGradDescent — Type
MonotoneAccProxGradDescent(; β = 0.5, α = 0.9, δ = 1e-3)

The monotone accelerated proximal gradient method of Li and Lin (Accelerated proximal gradient methods for nonconvex programming, NeurIPS 2015), for a smooth f and a proximable g that may be nonconvex, such as IndBallL0, IndBallRank, NormL0, MCP or SCAD. Each iteration takes a proximal gradient step from the extrapolated point

y = x + (t'/t) (z - x) + ((t' - 1)/t) (x - x'),

where z is the previous step from y and x' the previous iterate, and keeps its result z if F(z) ≤ F(x) - δ L ‖z - y‖², with F = f + g and 1/L the step size. Otherwise it also takes a plain proximal gradient step v from x and keeps the better of z and v. Under valid majorization and exact proximal updates, the objective does not increase in exact arithmetic. The cited critical-point and accelerated-rate results require their additional smoothness, step-size and boundedness assumptions; traits and a successful numerical line search do not establish them. A stopping flag does not certify global optimality for nonconvex g.

The step sizes come from backtracking as in AccProxGradDescent: L is multiplied by α ∈ (0, 1] before each iteration and divided by β ∈ (0, 1) until f(z) ≤ f(y) + ⟨∇f(y), z - y⟩ + L/2 ‖z - y‖². A point where f is not finite, such as a matrix off the positive definite cone for LogDetLoss, is never kept. Use with solve!.

source
ProximalSolvers.ActiveAccProxGradDescent — Type
ActiveAccProxGradDescent(; β = 0.5, α = 0.9, max_add = 100, growth = 2, max_outer = 1000)

Active-set accelerated proximal gradient descent, for f(x) + g(x) with a sparse minimizer, such as the lasso or the group lasso. The active set is the set of stored entries of a SparseIterate, or the set of active groups of an AtomIterate, whose coordinates are groups. Each outer iteration

  1. removes the coordinates that are zero from the active set (dropzeros!);
  2. computes the full gradient ∇f(x) and finds the coordinates outside the active set that violate the optimality conditions, kkt_violation > 0;
  3. stops if there are none and x minimizes f + g over the active set, that is, the last inner solve converged (or x = 0); an inner solve stopped by maxiter is thus repeated. It also stops after a converged inner solve that changed no entry of x: the next one would repeat it. This happens within rounding of the smallest penalty with a zero solution, where the check flags a coordinate that the proximal step leaves at zero;
  4. adds the violators to the active set (activate!), the largest violations first, until they hold max(max_add, (growth - 1) s) entries, where s is the number of entries left in the active set by step 1, and with them the violators whose violation equals the last one;
  5. minimizes f + g over the active set with AccProxGradDescent(; β, α), warm-started from the current values.

The limit in step 4 keeps the active set from filling up with the many coordinates that violate the optimality conditions at a poor starting point, such as zero for a small penalty: at each outer iteration the active set grows by a factor of at most growth over the nonzero entries of x, or by max_add entries if that is more. So it reaches a large support in a few outer iterations, but stays close to the support. growth = 1 adds max_add entries at a time, and max_add = typemax(Int) removes the limit.

Step 5 runs on restrict(f, S) and restrict(g, S) from ProximalBase, a problem in the active entries S only; step 2 is the only one that involves all coordinates. f and g need restrict, g needs kkt_violation and a full prox!, and f needs value_and_gradient! at the iterate: in ProximalBase these are, among others, QuadraticFunction, SqrNormL2, LeastSquaresLoss and GLMLoss, with NormL1, GroupNormL2, ZeroFunction and the other separable penalties.

For the group lasso GroupNormL2, an AtomIterate that shares the groups of g, AtomIterate(x, g.groups), makes the groups the coordinates: a group joins and leaves the active set whole. A dense x takes this route when the groups of g cover all coordinates. With a SparseIterate, the coordinates of a group at zero share the violation of the group, so a group that violates the optimality conditions joins the active set whole too.

Success also requires the full proximal fixed-point residual to be at most options.gradtol * max(1, norm(initial_gradient, Inf)). A repeated inner solution that fails this check returns converged = false.

max_outer limits the number of outer iterations. The ProximalOptions apply to each inner solve. Use with solve!, which returns an ActiveSetResults.

ProximalSolvers.SLOPEAPG — Type
SLOPEAPG(; maxiter=10_000, tol=1e-7)

Accelerated proximal gradient with SLOPE working sets and full optimality repairs for single-response LeastSquaresLoss, optionally Scaled. Call solve!(x, f, SLOPE(lambda), SLOPEAPG()) or solve_path(f, SLOPE(lambda), factors, SLOPEAPG(); screening=true). The loss and penalty are used in their supplied units; normalization, intercepts, standardization and statistical weight calibration belong to the caller.

Reduced fits use AccProxGradDescent. Each is checked on the full problem by norm(x - prox_g(x - gradient(f,x)), Inf), including the zero cluster. Convergence requires this residual to be at most tol * max(1, norm(gradient(f,zero(x)), Inf)). Violating excluded coordinates are added and refitted. A path uses the sequential SLOPE strong rule of Larsson, Bogdan and Wallin (2020), with these checks and repairs. The total inner iteration budget is maxiter per level, including repairs. The last single iteration of a budget cannot start an APG solve, which requires at least two.

This is a screened APG driver; it performs no cluster-coordinate updates. Returns SLOPEResults or SLOPEPathResults. It accepts dense floating-point coefficient vectors and dense or sparse designs, with no data conversion or statistical preprocessing. The loss data and starting coefficients must have the same floating-point element type.

ProximalSolvers.SLOPEHybrid — Type
SLOPEHybrid(; maxiter=10_000, tol=1e-7, cd_sweeps=10, stall_cycles=2)

Opt-in cluster-coordinate / proximal-gradient solver for single-response least squares with a SLOPE penalty. Uses the same paths, screening, full optimality checks and working-set repairs as SLOPEAPG. APG remains the default choice. Call solve!(x,f,g,SLOPEHybrid()) or solve_path(f,g,factors,SLOPEHybrid()) explicitly.

One PG step is followed by up to cd_sweeps exact nonzero-cluster sweeps. Cluster ordering, membership and scratch are reused; at most 32 signed cluster columns are cached, bounding dense cache storage even for a sparse input. Cached values are refreshed after PG and invalidated by relevant cluster changes. PG steps can split clusters and activate zeros.

If the reduced fixed-point residual fails to decrease by 20% for stall_cycles successive cycles, or a cluster block is nonfinite or increases the objective, that reduced solve switches permanently to APG. A bounded hybrid trial also reserves at least half the remaining iteration budget for APG. The full residual determines convergence, including after fallback. maxiter counts PG/APG steps and complete CD sweeps, summed over working-set repairs at each level.

This numerical option performs no preprocessing or statistical calibration. Dense and CSC designs have specialized allocation-free column accumulation; other matrices must provide efficient multiplication and column restriction.

ProximalSolvers.LineSearches.BackTracking — Type
BackTracking(; α = 0.9, β = 0.5, Lexact = Inf)

The backtracking line search of TFOCS (private/tfocs_backtrack.m). Each iteration starts from L ← α L, where 1/L is the step size, and while the local Lipschitz estimate L̂ of the step exceeds L it retries with L ← min(Lexact, max(L̂, L / β)). L̂ comes from function values until they are too close to compare in floating point, and from gradients after that. A trial point where f is not finite, such as a matrix off the positive definite cone for LogDetLoss, is never accepted: it gives no estimate, and the search retries with L ← L / β.

The object holds settings only and can be shared between solves; the bookkeeping lives in the solver state.

The ADMM solver

ProximalSolvers.ADMMSolver — Type
ADMMSolver(; ρ = 1.0, α = 1.0, abstol = 1e-6, reltol = 1e-4, adaptive = false,
             abstol_dual = abstol, norm = nothing)

The alternating direction method of multipliers for f(x) + g(x) when both f and g have easy proximal operators (Boyd et al., Distributed Optimization and Statistical Learning via the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers, 2011). It splits x = z and iterates

x ← prox_{f/ρ}(z - u)
z ← prox_{g/ρ}(α x + (1 - α) z + u)
u ← u + α x + (1 - α) z_previous - z

ρ > 0 is the augmented Lagrangian parameter and α ∈ (0, 2) the over-relaxation (α = 1 is plain ADMM; 1.5 to 1.8 are common choices). The usual convergence result requires closed proper convex functions, exact proximal updates and a saddle point of the split Lagrangian. A successful residual test is not a general convergence guarantee for nonconvex functions. It stops when the primal residual ‖x - z‖ is below √n abstol + reltol ⋅ max(‖x‖, ‖z‖) and the dual residual ρ‖z - z_previous‖ below `√n abstol_dual

  • reltol ⋅ ρ‖u‖(§3.3 of the paper). Withadaptive,ρ` balances the two

residuals (§3.4.1): it doubles when the primal residual, relative to its tolerance, is more than ten times the dual one, halves in the opposite case, and stays fixed after 50 changes, which keeps the convergence guarantee of a fixed ρ.

x is an Array, or a tuple of arrays for a problem over several blocks: with f = SeparableSum(f1, …, fK) the x-update is K independent proximal steps, and g may couple the blocks, as the fused graphical lasso does for a pair of precision matrices. n is the total number of entries, and a tuple of one array computes exactly what the array does.

norm is the norm in which the residuals and ‖x‖, ‖z‖ and ‖u‖ are measured, a function of an iterate (an array, or a tuple of arrays); nothing is the Euclidean norm, over all the blocks of a tuple. The K blocks of a stacked array, for example, can be measured by the sum of the norms of its slices, x -> sum(k -> LinearAlgebra.norm(view(x, :, :, k)), axes(x, 3)), and those of a tuple by x -> sum(LinearAlgebra.norm, x). If norm also has a method norm(x, y) for the norm of x - y, the residuals are measured with it, without forming the differences.

Of the ProximalOptions, only maxiter, time_limit, prox_calls_limit, show_trace, print_every, gaptol and gapevery are used. With gaptol > 0 and functions that have a duality gap (see duality_gap; among them the graphical lasso, a LogDetLoss with a NormL1), the gap at z replaces the residual tests. Use with solve!; to warm start from a previous result res, pass its scaled dual variable and final penalty parameter: solve!(x, f, g, ADMMSolver(; ρ = res.ρ), options; u = res.u).

ProximalSolvers.CondatVu — Type
CondatVu(; τ = nothing, σ = nothing, norm_d = nothing, L = nothing,
           abstol = 1e-6, reltol = 1e-4, adaptive = true, restart = true,
           checkevery = 25, max_adaptations = 50)

Primal–dual splitting for convex f(x) + g(x) + h(D*x). Use solve!(x, f, g, h, D, method, options; y = nothing, gap = nothing). f is smooth with a globally Lipschitz gradient; g and h are proximable. x is a floating vector (including SparseIterate), and D a real matrix. The dual proximal step uses Conjugate(h) and cached Moreau buffers.

Steps satisfy τ*(L/2 + σ*norm_d^2) < 1. L defaults to lipschitz(f). Some lipschitz implementations, including least squares, return a numerical estimate; supply a known L bound when a certified step condition is needed. norm_d defaults to the guaranteed matrix bound √(opnorm(D, 1)*opnorm(D, Inf)); for faster steps, supply a tighter known bound. ProximalBase's Lanczos estimate sqrt(opnorm2(D)) can guide that choice, but is not a certified upper bound. Explicit steps are checked against the supplied bounds. Automatic steps have a 0.99 safety factor.

Both KKT residuals must meet absolute plus relative tolerances; small iterate changes alone do not certify convergence. adaptive balances normalized residuals, and restart compares the current point with the running average using a proximal residual check every checkevery iterations. Restarts use PDLP's sufficient-decrease, stalled-decrease and long-epoch criteria and its smoothed primal-weight update (Applegate et al., 2021; also used by CovarianceSelection's CLIME PDHG). These are heuristics for general convex composites, not PDLP's LP convergence-rate theorem. At most max_adaptations changes/restarts are allowed, after which fixed-step Condat–Vũ convergence applies under the stated bounds.

Ordinary iterations use one product each by D and D', two proximal calls and one gradient call. Restart checks cost additional products and proximal calls, all included in the result's counters. No matrices are factorized. Float32 iterates keep Float32 buffers and steps.

Warm start with CondatVu(; τ = res.τ, σ = res.σ, ...) and y = res.y; x and a supplied y are overwritten. res aliases those arrays.

Of ProximalOptions, the iteration/time/call limits, tracing and callback are used. gaptol > 0 selects a user-supplied relative duality gap gap(x, y) as the stopping rule, evaluated every gapevery iterations and at return. By default this uses duality_gap(f, g, h, D, x, y) when its conjugate values are available. A custom callback must provide a valid certificate for this composite problem. With gaptol = 0, residuals are used. Gap computations and user callbacks are excluded from the evaluation counters. ftol, xtol, gradtol and allow_f_increases are not used.

Duality gaps and optimality

ProximalSolvers.duality_gap — Function
duality_gap(f, g, h, D, x, y) -> (gap, primal, dual)

A Fenchel certificate for convex f(x) + g(x) + h(D*x) at primal/dual vectors x,y. Requires the relevant conjugate values in ProximalBase. For g = ZeroFunction() and a squared Euclidean loss, the dual is -f*(-D'y) - h*(y). Otherwise set u = ∇f(x) and use f*(u) = dot(x,u) - f(x) and the dual -f*(u) - g*(-u-D'y) - h*(y). An infeasible dual point gives an infinite gap. When f = ZeroFunction() and g = NormL1(...), scale y toward zero so -D'y lies in the domain of g*; this also preserves feasibility of a translated/scaled check loss. This does not change the returned solver dual. The routine allocates working vectors; it is intended for occasional checks.

source
duality_gap(f, g, x) -> (gap, primal, dual)

The duality gap primal - dual of minimizing f + g at x, where primal is f(x) + g(x) and dual the value of the Fenchel dual at a dual point built from the gradient at x, so that 0 ≤ primal - min(f + g) ≤ gap: a certificate of the accuracy of x, which is zero at the solution. The dual point is θ = t ∇F(Ax) for f(x) = F(Ax), scaled by t ∈ (0, 1] into the domain of the conjugate of g (Fercoq, Gramfort and Salmon, 2015).

Available for:

  • f a LeastSquaresLoss with a vector y, or a GLMLoss with the squared, logistic, Poisson or Huber loss, possibly scaled, and g one of NormL1, NormL1Nonneg and ElasticNet with positive weights, or a GroupNormL2, SparseGroupNormL2 or Scaled(SparseGroupNormL2(α, 1 - α, groups, w), λ) whose groups cover x, with positive weights for the GroupNormL2. Here x is a vector, a SparseIterate or, for the group penalties, an AtomIterate. A coordinate without penalty has no dual bound, so with one the gap exists only where its gradient is zero.
  • f a CDModel of such a loss, as from cdmodel, for instance with an intercept; duality_gap initializes it at x.
  • the graphical lasso: f a LogDetLoss, g a NormL1 (with a matrix of weights, for example a zero diagonal) and x a symmetric matrix. The dual point is W - S clipped to the penalty, with W = x⁻¹, at the cost of two Cholesky factorizations.

An ArgumentError is thrown for other functions; see kkt_residual for an optimality measure that needs no conjugate.

source
ProximalSolvers.kkt_residual — Function
kkt_residual(f, g, x; relative = true)

The largest scaled change √aₖ ⋅ |Δxₖ| that one coordinate step of CDSolver would make at x, over all coordinates, where aₖ is the curvature of f along coordinate k and the step is the proximal step from coordinate_derivatives. When both are convex and f is differentiable at x, it is zero exactly at a minimizer of f + g. The square-root models do not supply the coordinate derivatives required by this diagnostic; inspect the gap in CDSolver's results for those losses. It is Inf along a coordinate with zero curvature where f + g is unbounded below. It is the quantity that the stopping rule of CDSolver compares with tol: with relative, it is divided by the scale of the data (response_scale), as there. x is not changed.

It applies to the losses that cdmodel knows and that define coordinate_derivatives (LeastSquaresLoss, GLMLoss, QuadraticFunction, KronQuadratic), or to a CDModel, on a SparseIterate or a SymmetricSparseIterate, with any separable penalty. For the losses without a duality gap (see duality_gap), such as the quadratics, it is the optimality measure to use.

The symmetric-iterate parameter rule of solve! also applies here.
