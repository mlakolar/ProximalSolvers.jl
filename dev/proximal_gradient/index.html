Proximal gradient methods

ProximalGradientDescent and AccProxGradDescent minimize $F = f + g$ for a smooth convex $f$ and a convex $g$ whose proximal operator,

\[\operatorname{prox}_{\gamma g}(v) = \operatorname*{argmin}_z \; g(z) + \frac{1}{2\gamma}\|z - v\|^2 ,\]

is cheap to compute: soft thresholding for the ℓ1 norm, block soft thresholding for the group lasso, soft thresholding of the singular values for the nuclear norm, a projection for the indicator function of a set. They need two oracles, the gradient of $f$ and the proximal operator of $g$, so they take any pair of functions of ProximalBase that provide them.

The proximal gradient step

With a step $1/L$, the step from $x$ minimizes $g$ plus the quadratic model of $f$ at $x$:

\[x^+ = \operatorname*{argmin}_z \; f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), z - x\rangle + \frac{L}{2}\|z - x\|^2 + g(z) = \operatorname{prox}_{g/L}\Big(x - \frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x)\Big).\]

Write $Q_L(z; x) = f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), z - x\rangle + \frac{L}{2}\|z - x\|^2$. If the upper bound

\[f(x^+) \le Q_L(x^+; x)\]

holds, the step decreases $F$: since $x^+$ minimizes $Q_L(\cdot\,; x) + g$, which is $L$-strongly convex, $F(x^+) \le Q_L(x^+; x) + g(x^+) \le F(x) - \tfrac{L}{2}\|x^+ - x\|^2$. The bound holds for every $x$ when $L$ is at least the Lipschitz constant $L_f$ of $\nabla f$, and then the iterates satisfy $F(x_k) - F^\star \le L\|x_0 - x^\star\|^2/(2k)$ (Beck and Teboulle, 2009). The solvers do not need $L_f$: they find a step by backtracking, which also adapts to the local curvature, often much smaller than $L_f$.

The fixed points of the step are the minimizers. The optimality condition $0 \in \nabla f(x) + \partial g(x)$ is equivalent to $x = \operatorname{prox}_{g/L}(x - \nabla f(x)/L)$ for any $L > 0$.

Proximal gradient descent

ProximalGradientDescent takes the step above with the backtracking line search of TFOCS (Becker, Candès and Grant, 2011), the BackTracking(; α = 0.9, β = 0.5, Lexact = Inf) of ProximalSolvers.LineSearches. Each iteration

  1. lowers the curvature estimate, $L \leftarrow \alpha L$, so that the step can grow where the curvature is smaller;
  2. takes the step $x^+ = \operatorname{prox}_{g/L}(x - \nabla f(x)/L)$;
  3. computes a local Lipschitz estimate $\hat L$ along $d = x^+ - x$;
  4. accepts the step if $\hat L \le L$ (or $L \ge L_{\text{exact}}$), and otherwise sets $L \leftarrow \min(L_{\text{exact}}, \max(\hat L, L/\beta))$ and returns to step 2.

The estimate $\hat L$ comes from function values while they can be compared in floating point:

\[\hat L = L + \frac{2\,\max\{f(x^+) - Q_L(x^+; x),\, 0\}}{\|d\|^2},\]

the smallest $L'$ with $f(x^+) \le Q_{L'}(x^+; x)$. Near the solution $f(x^+)$ and $f(x)$ agree to almost all their digits, and the difference $f(x^+) - Q_L$ is lost to cancellation. Once $|f(x) - f(x^+)| < 10^{-10}\max\{|f(x)|, |f(x^+)|\}$, the estimate switches for good to one from gradients,

\[\hat L = \frac{2\,\langle \nabla f(x^+) - \nabla f(x),\, d\rangle}{\|d\|^2}.\]

For convex $f$, $f(x^+) - f(x) - \langle\nabla f(x), d\rangle \le \langle \nabla f(x^+) - \nabla f(x), d\rangle$, so $\hat L \le L$ implies the upper bound $f(x^+) \le Q_L(x^+; x)$. The second estimate costs a gradient at the trial point, which the next iteration needs anyway.

Points off the domain. A loss can be infinite on part of the space, as $\operatorname{tr}(S\Theta) - \log\det\Theta$ is off the positive definite cone. A trial point where $f$ is not finite gives no estimate and is never accepted: the search sets $L \leftarrow L/\beta$ and tries again, past $L_{\text{exact}}$ if need be. A small enough step from a point of the domain stays in it, so the search ends. The starting point must be in the domain: both methods throw an ArgumentError when $f(x_0)$ is not finite, and the identity is a valid start for the log-determinant loss.

Accelerated proximal gradient descent

AccProxGradDescent takes the proximal gradient step from an extrapolated point (Nesterov's acceleration, FISTA of Beck and Teboulle, 2009, in the form of §4.3 of Parikh and Boyd, 2014):

\[y = x_k + \omega_k (x_k - x_{k-1}), \qquad \omega_k = \frac{k}{k + 3}, \qquad x_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{g/L}\Big(y - \frac{1}{L}\nabla f(y)\Big),\]

where $k$ counts the iterations since the last restart, so the first step after a restart has no momentum. The step size comes from backtracking on the upper bound at $y$: starting from $\alpha L$, $L$ is divided by $\beta$ until

\[f(x_{k+1}) \le f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y), x_{k+1} - y\rangle + \frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1} - y\|^2 + 10\,\varepsilon \max\{|f(y)|, 1\},\]

with $\varepsilon$ the machine precision; the last term keeps rounding near the solution from forcing endless backtracking. With $\alpha = 1$, $L$ never decreases, and without restarts this is the accelerated method with backtracking of Beck and Teboulle (2009), whose objective converges at the rate $O(1/k^2)$, against $O(1/k)$ without momentum. The default $\alpha = 0.9$ lets the step grow again, as in TFOCS. The number and cost of backtracking trials depend on the problem; neither setting is uniformly faster.

Restarts. Momentum makes the objective oscillate when the extrapolation overshoots. Following the function-value scheme of O'Donoghue and Candès (2015), when $F(x_{k+1}) > F(x_k)$ the iteration is repeated with $\omega = 0$ from $y = x_k$, and $k$ starts again at zero. A step from $x_k$ itself cannot increase $F$, so this happens at most once per iteration, and the objective is nonincreasing. An extrapolated point off the domain of $f$, where no step from it exists, restarts the iteration in the same way; a trial point off the domain fails the upper bound, so backtracking shortens the step.

Nonconvex penalties

Both methods also take a $g$ that is not convex but has a cheap proximal operator, such as the indicator of the ℓ0 ball IndBallL0(s), IndBallRank, NormL0, MCP or SCAD; its proximal operator is then a minimizer of a nonconvex problem. The step from $x$ still minimizes $Q_L(\cdot\,; x) + g$, so under the upper bound $F(x^+) \le Q_L(x^+; x) + g(x^+) \le Q_L(x; x) + g(x) = F(x)$: the objective of proximal gradient descent does not increase in exact arithmetic. A limit-point stationarity result also requires a proper lower semicontinuous penalty, suitable smoothness and bounded-step conditions, and bounded iterates; the function traits do not establish these conditions. With g = IndBallL0(s) it is iterative hard thresholding with a line search, and ProximalGradientDescent(; normalized = false, debias = 0) has two options from that literature:

  • normalized = true starts the backtracking of each iteration from the normalized step of Blumensath and Davies (2010) instead of from $\alpha L$. With $d$ the gradient at $x$ on the support of $x$ (all of it when $x = 0$) and $\tau = 1/L$, a quadratic $f$ has $\langle d, \nabla^2 f\, d\rangle = 2\big(f(x - \tau d) - f(x) + \tau\|d\|^2\big)/\tau^2$, and the search starts from $L = \langle d, \nabla^2 f\, d\rangle / \|d\|^2$, whose step $1/L$ minimizes $f$ along $d$. It costs one value of $f$, and keeps $L$ when the estimate is not positive and finite.
  • debias = k > 0 refits $f$ on the support of $x$ with refit! once the support has not changed for $k$ iterations, and keeps the refit if it lowers $F$, as MendelIHT does. The refit keeps the support, so it stays feasible for an ℓ0 or rank constraint, and each support is refitted once. It needs a loss that refit! takes and a vector, SparseIterate or SymmetricSparseIterate iterate.

The restarts of AccProxGradDescent keep its objective from increasing for a nonconvex $g$ too, but its iterates need not approach a critical point. MonotoneAccProxGradDescent is the monotone method of Li and Lin (2015, Algorithm 2). With $t_0 = 0$, $t_1 = 1$ and $t_{k+1} = (1 + \sqrt{4t_k^2 + 1})/2$, each iteration takes a proximal gradient step from an extrapolated point,

\[y_k = x_k + \frac{t_{k-1}}{t_k}(z_k - x_k) + \frac{t_{k-1} - 1}{t_k}(x_k - x_{k-1}), \qquad z_{k+1} = \operatorname{prox}_{g/L}\Big(y_k - \frac{1}{L}\nabla f(y_k)\Big),\]

with the backtracking of AccProxGradDescent on the upper bound at $y_k$. It keeps $x_{k+1} = z_{k+1}$ when $F(z_{k+1}) \le F(x_k) - \delta L\|z_{k+1} - y_k\|^2$, by default with $\delta = 10^{-3}$. Otherwise it also takes a plain step from $x_k$, with its own backtracking, and keeps the better of the two. So the objective never increases in exact arithmetic under the accepted majorization bounds. The cited stationarity and accelerated-rate results require their additional assumptions; numerical backtracking, restarts and approximate proximal operators do not establish those results by themselves. An iteration that falls back costs a second gradient and a second search.

For least-squares and generalized linear losses, including scaled losses, the method caches the images of the iterates under the design matrix. The image of $y_k$ is the same combination of the images of $x_k$, $z_k$ and $x_{k-1}$, so it needs no new product. A trial point costs one product with the design and a gradient one product with its transpose; the plain fallback reuses the image of $x_k$. Both candidate images are kept because the next extrapolation uses $z_k$ even when the plain candidate was selected. Each candidate image comes from a direct product, which prevents accumulated drift. Other smooth losses and tuple iterates use the original value and gradient oracles.

Sparse plus low-rank block iterates

All three gradient methods accept tuples of arrays, including MonotoneAccProxGradDescent. The blocks can have different shapes and storage: a SymmetricSparseIterate for a sparse symmetric matrix S and a dense rectangular factor U, for example. They use one backtracked step size for the whole pair and the Euclidean norm across both blocks.

On a SymmetricSparseIterate, the three gradient solvers prepare $g$ by the same parameter rule as coordinate descent: they average parameters that enter linearly and require the other per-entry parameters to be symmetric. For a tuple with a SeparableSum penalty, the rule applies block by block. Penalties that couple blocks pass unchanged; their compatibility with symmetric storage is the caller's responsibility. The smooth loss $f$ is unchanged.

For a latent graphical model, write Θ = S + U * U' and supply a smooth loss with

\[f(S,U) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma\Theta)-\log\det\Theta,\qquad G = \Sigma-\Theta^{-1},\quad \nabla_S f=G,\quad \nabla_U f=2GU.\]

The loss must test a Cholesky factorization of Θ before taking its inverse or log determinant. Return Inf when factorization fails, including from value_and_gradient!, and leave gradient buffers unused at that point. The line search rejects these trials. With this loss f, the solver call is

x = (SymmetricSparseIterate(Matrix(1.0I, p, p)), copy(U0))
g = SeparableSum(IndBallL0(s; diagonal = false), ZeroFunction())
r = solve!(x, f, g, MonotoneAccProxGradDescent())

The same interface applies to a D-trace loss composed with S + U * U'. Its gradient in S is the symmetric part of the matrix gradient, and the factor gradient is twice that symmetric part times U. The sign of the low-rank term is part of the supplied model; a solver does not choose it.

For ProximalGradientDescent(; normalized = true), support restriction is applied to every block. The unrestricted gradient is used only when the entire tuple is zero. A zero block beside an active block remains excluded from the curvature trial. For a nonlinear coupled loss this curvature estimate is a trial heuristic; backtracking still checks the original loss. Tuple debiasing is not supported: debias > 0 requires a single iterate supported by refit!.

The symmetric projection requires ProximalBase 0.5.3. When the diagonal counts toward the sparsity budget, an off-diagonal pair is ranked by its Frobenius weight √2 * abs(S[i,j]). This can select a different support from a legacy routine that ranks every stored entry by magnitude, even without ties. These nonconvex problems can also have different local solutions; a lower objective does not establish a global optimum.

Stopping rules

The methods stop at the first of these tests to pass, after the iteration limit, the time limit or a limit on the number of calls, when the callback returns true, or when the objective increases (which rounding can cause near the solution) unless allow_f_increases is set. With $x'$ the previous iterate and $F = f + g$:

  • ftol, the relative change of the objective: $|F(x) - F(x')| / (|F(x)| + \mathrm{ftol}) < \mathrm{ftol}$, or a change below the rounding error of $|F(x)| + |F(x')|$.
  • xtol, the change of the iterate: $\|x - x'\| < \mathrm{xtol}\cdot\max\{\|x\|, 1\}$.
  • gradtol, the residual of the optimality condition, as in FASTA (Goldstein, Studer and Baraniuk, 2014).

The residual comes from the step itself. For proximal gradient descent, the optimality condition of the step from $x'$ with step size $1/L$ says that $v = L(x' - x) - \nabla f(x')$ is a subgradient of $g$ at $x$. So

\[r = \nabla f(x) + v = \nabla f(x) - \nabla f(x') - L(x - x') \in \nabla f(x) + \partial g(x),\]

and $r = 0$ exactly when $x$ is a minimizer. The accelerated method steps from $y$ and never evaluates $\nabla f(x)$. Its residual $\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y) - L(x - y)$ is at most $(L + L_f)\|x - y\|$, and $L\|x - y\|_\infty$ stands in for it; for the monotone method, $y$ is the point its kept step came from, $y_k$ or $x_k$. The test passes when either the relative residual or the normalized residual is below gradtol:

\[\frac{\|r\|_\infty}{\max_{j \le k}\|r_j\|_\infty + \mathrm{gradtol}} < \mathrm{gradtol} \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{\|r\|_\infty}{\max\{\|\nabla f\|_\infty, \|v\|_\infty\} + \mathrm{gradtol}} < \mathrm{gradtol},\]

with the gradient at $x$ (at $y$ for the accelerated method). The first compares the residual with the largest one seen so far; the second with the size of the two terms that cancel at the solution.

With gaptol > 0 and functions that have a duality gap, the relative gap, computed every gapevery iterations, replaces these tests and the stop on an increasing objective; see Duality gaps.

Choosing tolerances. Near a minimizer at which $F$ grows quadratically, $F(x) - F^\star$ is of the order of $\|x - x^\star\|^2$, so the objective can settle before the iterate does. An objective-change threshold such as ftol = 1e-12 does not by itself guarantee any number of correct coefficient digits: the change between iterations is not the error relative to the optimum. When accurate solutions matter, tighten gradtol and xtol as well, or check the optimality conditions of the problem afterwards, as the examples do.

Sparse iterates

Both methods keep the type of the starting point. With a SparseIterate (or, for the group lasso, an AtomIterate with the groups of the penalty), zeros are dropped after every step, and the products of a least-squares loss by the iterate cost $O(n)$ per stored entry or group. For a sparse solution with many coordinates, Active-set proximal gradient goes further and works on the active coordinates only.
