Primal–dual splitting

For a derivation, harder worked problems, and comparisons with QP and ordinary or specialized ADMM methods, read Choosing a splitting for a composite problem. That lecture explains when this solver is useful and when a specialized proximal operator or a different splitting is preferable.

CondatVu minimizes

\[f(x)+g(x)+h(Dx),\]

where the three functions are convex, f has a globally Lipschitz gradient, and g and h have proximal operators. D is a dense or sparse real matrix. The primal iterate is a floating vector or a SparseIterate; the dual is a dense vector of length size(D, 1).

res = solve!(x, f, g, h, D, CondatVu(), ProximalOptions(maxiter = 100_000))

The iteration is

\[\begin{aligned} x^+ &= \operatorname{prox}_{\tau g}(x-\tau(\nabla f(x)+D^\top y)),\\ y^+ &= \operatorname{prox}_{\sigma h^*}(y+\sigma D(2x^+-x)). \end{aligned}\]

ProximalBase's Conjugate(h) computes the second proximal operator by Moreau's identity, retaining its scratch buffer. Cached gradients and products allow one multiplication by each of D and its adjoint per ordinary iteration. Restart and gap checks perform additional operations.

Step sizes and adaptation

The condition is τ*(L/2 + σ*norm_d^2) < 1, where L bounds the Lipschitz constant of ∇f and norm_d bounds the spectral norm of D. The defaults use lipschitz(f) and sqrt(opnorm(D, 1)*opnorm(D, Inf)), without densifying a sparse matrix. Supply known tighter bounds through CondatVu(; L, norm_d). Some lipschitz implementations, including least squares, return numerical estimates; supply a certified L when the formal step guarantee is needed. Lanczos norm estimates can help choose steps experimentally, but are not certified upper bounds. Explicit τ and σ are checked against these bounds. With f = ZeroFunction(), the method is Chambolle–Pock.

By default, every 25 iterations the solver compares the current iterate with an average since the last restart, using a proximal KKT residual. It applies the sufficient-decrease, stalled-decrease and long-epoch restart criteria used by PDLP and CovarianceSelection's CLIME PDHG. At a restart, it smooths the primal weight from the primal and dual distances traveled. The new step pair continues to satisfy the Condat–Vũ condition. restart = false instead uses normalized residual balancing when adaptive = true.

These rules are heuristics for general composites. The solver makes at most max_adaptations = 50 changes, then continues with fixed steps; the fixed-step convergence result requires the stated convexity and global bounds. Use adaptive = false, restart = false for fixed steps throughout. No Malitsky–Pock line search or generalization of PDLP's LP rate theorem is claimed.

Checking and reusing a solution

res.r_norm and res.s_norm measure the two KKT inclusions, using the subgradients supplied by the proximal updates. Each is compared with an absolute plus relative tolerance. The primal residual scales by the largest of the gradient, adjoint product and proximal subgradient norms. The dual residual scales by the largest of norm(D*x) and its proximal subgradient norm. This prevents tiny steps from passing merely because the iterates move little. Set abstol and reltol in CondatVu.

With ProximalOptions(; gaptol > 0), a relative Fenchel gap replaces the residual stopping rule. The six-argument duality_gap evaluates conjugate values, using dual scaling for a zero smooth term and an ℓ1 penalty. An infeasible dual gives an infinite gap. Unsupported conjugates raise an error; alternatively supply a valid relative gap(x, y) callback. The final returned pair is checked even when a limit falls between scheduled checks. Gap evaluation allocates working vectors and is excluded from the oracle counters, as are user callbacks.

Warm start both iterates and the final step sizes:

method = CondatVu(τ = res.τ, σ = res.σ, L = L, norm_d = norm_d)
next = solve!(res.minimizer, f, next_g, h, D, method, options; y = res.y)

Both arrays are overwritten, so copy them if the preceding result must be preserved. Step sizes must satisfy the bounds of the new problem.

Quantile regression and generalized lasso

For quantile level q, observations b and design X, use

f = ZeroFunction()
g = NormL1(λ)
h = Scaled(Translate(CheckLoss(1 - q), b), 1 / length(b))
res = solve!(zeros(size(X, 2)), f, g, h, X, CondatVu(),
             ProximalOptions(maxiter = 500_000, gaptol = 1e-6))
centered_scores = -length(b) .* res.y
rank_scores = centered_scores .+ (1 - q)

The sign follows from h(z) = mean(ρ_q(b-z)): res.y is the subgradient with respect to predictions. Centered residual scores lie in [q-1, q]; adding 1-q gives dual rank-score coordinates in [0,1]. Penalization changes their moment constraints. For rank-score tests, check dual accuracy at the required fixed quantile; objective accuracy alone does not certify individual dual coordinates. The benchmark includes small LP comparisons of coefficients and duals. LPs can be faster at moderate sizes.

For generalized lasso, set f to a least-squares loss, g to the coefficient penalty (or ZeroFunction()), h = NormL1(λ), and D to a sparse difference or incidence matrix. The example below uses second differences. For ordinary chain TV denoising, ProximalBase's direct TotalVariation1D proximal operator is the faster available route.

See benchmark/primal_dual for the oracle checks and comparison with ProximalAlgorithms through the existing adapter. Neither package is a runtime dependency.
